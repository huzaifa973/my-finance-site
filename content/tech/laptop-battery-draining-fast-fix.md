Your laptop battery used to last a full workday and now it dies before lunch. Before you rush out to buy a replacement battery or a whole new laptop, work through these nine fixes. Most fast-draining batteries are caused by software and settings — runaway background apps, a screen cranked to full brightness, or a dozen browser tabs — not by the battery itself. Even when the battery is genuinely worn, these steps will squeeze out more life and tell you exactly when a replacement is actually worth it.
## 9 fixes for a laptop battery that drains too fast
**1. Turn down screen brightness.** The display is almost always the single biggest battery drain on any laptop. On both Windows and Mac, drop brightness to 50–60% for indoor use — most people cannot tell the difference, but battery life improves dramatically. Enable auto-brightness so the screen dims in darker rooms. On Windows, go to **Settings > System > Display** and turn on **Change brightness automatically**. On Mac, go to **System Settings > Displays** and enable **Automatically adjust brightness**.
**2. Turn on battery saver mode.** Both operating systems have a dedicated power-saving mode that throttles background activity, reduces screen brightness slightly, and pauses syncing. On Windows, click the battery icon in the taskbar and slide to **Best battery life**, or go to **Settings > System > Power & battery** and set Battery saver to turn on automatically at 50%. On Mac, go to **System Settings > Battery** and enable **Low Power Mode**. You will lose a little performance but gain significant battery life.
**3. Kill background apps and startup programs.** Apps you are not even looking at are quietly draining your battery. On Windows, press **Ctrl+Shift+Esc** to open Task Manager, go to the **Processes** tab, and end tasks for apps you do not need — especially game launchers, cloud sync tools, and updaters. Go to the **Startup** tab and disable everything you do not need at boot. On Mac, go to **System Settings > General > Login Items** and remove apps you do not need launching at startup. Also check **Activity Monitor** (in Applications > Utilities) and quit any process using high CPU.
**4. Turn off the keyboard backlight.** Keyboard backlights look great but consume noticeable power, especially on gaming laptops with RGB lighting. Set the backlight to turn off automatically after 5–10 seconds of inactivity — on most laptops this is in the keyboard settings or via a function key. If you mostly type in well-lit rooms, turn it off entirely. Also disable any RGB lighting on the laptop body or gaming peripherals.
**5. Close browser tabs and use a power-efficient browser.** A browser with 30 open tabs can consume more CPU and battery than any other app on your laptop. Close tabs you are not using, and use a tab-suspender extension that unloads inactive tabs automatically. Microsoft Edge and Safari are generally more battery-efficient than Chrome; if battery life matters more than Chrome extensions, consider switching. In Chrome, go to **Settings > Performance** and enable **Memory Saver** and **Energy Saver**.
**6. Unplug USB peripherals and disable unused radios.** Every USB device plugged into your laptop — external drives, mice, dongles, phone charging cables — draws power from the battery. Unplug anything you are not actively using. Also turn off Wi-Fi and Bluetooth when you do not need them (use airplane mode on flights), and on Windows go to **Settings > Network & Internet** to disable any virtual network adapters you are not using. On Mac, turn off Bluetooth in Control Center when not needed.
**7. Generate a battery health report (Windows) or check battery condition (Mac).** Before spending money on a replacement, find out the actual health of your battery. On Windows, right-click the Start menu, choose **Terminal (Admin)** or **Command Prompt (Admin)**, and type `powercfg /batteryreport` — press Enter, then open the generated HTML file. Look at **Design Capacity** versus **Full Charge Capacity**: if full charge is below 60–70% of design capacity, the battery is worn out. On Mac, go to **System Settings > Battery**, click the **i** icon next to Battery Health, and check the condition — "Normal" means it is fine, "Service Recommended" means replacement time. Also check the cycle count: most laptop batteries are rated for 500–1000 cycles.
**8. Calibrate the battery.** If your battery percentage jumps around or shuts down suddenly at 20–30%, it may need calibration — the battery's sensor and its actual charge are out of sync. To calibrate: charge the battery to 100% and leave it plugged in for two more hours. Then unplug it and use the laptop normally until it shuts down from low battery. Leave it off for 5 hours, then charge it back to 100% without interruption. Do this once every 2–3 months. On modern laptops with built-in calibration tools, follow the manufacturer's utility instead.
**9. Know when to replace the battery — or the laptop.** If your battery health report shows capacity below 60%, or the cycle count exceeds the rated number, a replacement will restore full runtime. Genuine batteries from the manufacturer or an authorized service center are worth the extra cost — third-party cheap batteries often have lower capacity and can be unsafe. However, if your laptop is more than 5–6 years old and also slow, putting a new battery into an otherwise aging machine may not be worth it; compare the battery price against the cost of a new or refurbished laptop.
## Frequently asked questions
**Is it bad to keep my laptop plugged in all the time?**
Modern laptops stop charging at 100% and run on AC power, so keeping it plugged in does not overcharge the battery. However, keeping the battery permanently at 100% while hot can accelerate wear. If you mostly use it plugged in, use the manufacturer's battery limiter (many brands cap at 60–80% for plugged-in use) to extend battery life.
**How long should a laptop battery last on a full charge?**
Most modern laptops get 6–10 hours of light use (web browsing, documents, video). Gaming laptops and older machines may get 3–5 hours. If you are getting less than half of what the laptop was rated for when new, and it is more than 2–3 years old, the battery is likely worn — check with the battery report in step 7.
**Does fast charging damage the battery?**
Occasional fast charging is fine, but frequent fast charging generates more heat, which accelerates battery degradation. For everyday charging at your desk, use a standard charger or the slow-charge mode if your laptop offers one. Avoid using the laptop heavily while it is charging, since that raises temperature further.
**Can I replace the battery myself?**
On many older and business laptops, yes — the battery is user-replaceable with a few screws. On modern ultrabooks and MacBooks, the battery is glued in and requires specialized tools and expertise. For those, use an authorized service center; a botched DIY replacement can damage the laptop or create a fire hazard.
