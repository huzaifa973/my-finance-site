Your laptop gets hot enough to burn your lap, the fan screams like a jet engine, and everything slows to a crawl. Overheating is the most common laptop problem after a year or two of use, and it is almost always fixable without buying a new machine. Heat forces your CPU and GPU to throttle (slow themselves down) to survive, which is why a hot laptop feels sluggish even though the specs are fine. Here are eight fixes that actually work, ordered from easiest to most advanced.

## 8 fixes that work

**1. Clean the dust out of your vents and fan.** Dust is the number one cause of laptop overheating. After months of sucking in air, the intake vents and the tiny fan fins get caked with lint that blocks airflow. Turn the laptop off, unplug it, and use a can of compressed air (held upright, short bursts) to blow through the vents on the bottom and sides. If you can open the bottom panel, blow the fan blades directly and wipe the heat sink fins with a cotton swab. You will be shocked at how much dust comes out — and how much cooler the laptop runs afterward.

**2. Buy a cooling pad.** A cooling pad with built-in fans sits under your laptop and blows extra air into the intake vents. It is the single cheapest upgrade you can make, and it can drop temperatures by 5 to 10 degrees Celsius under load. Look for a pad with fans that line up with your laptop's actual intake vents (check the bottom of your laptop to see where air goes in), and prefer USB-powered models with adjustable fan speed. Avoid pads that are just a flat piece of plastic with no airflow.

**3. Elevate the back of the laptop.** Most laptops suck air in through the bottom, so if it is sitting flat on a desk or — worse — a bed or couch, those vents get smothered. Prop the back edge up an inch or two with a laptop stand, a stack of books, or even a clean empty pencil case. This lets air circulate underneath and can lower idle temperatures by a few degrees for free. Never use a laptop on a soft surface like a blanket or pillow for long sessions; the fabric blocks vents and can even get sucked into the fan.

**4. Undervolt your CPU (advanced).** Undervolting means feeding your processor slightly less voltage than the factory default. It produces the same performance with less heat, and it is completely reversible. On Intel laptops, use the free tool ThroStop or Intel XTU; on AMD laptops, use Ryzen Controller. Lower the core voltage offset in small steps (start at -50mV), run a stress test for ten minutes, and keep going lower until the system crashes — then back off by 20mV. A proper undervolt can cut temperatures by 10 to 15 degrees under full load. If that sounds too technical, skip it — the other fixes will still help a lot.

**5. Switch to a balanced or power-saver plan.** Running your laptop on the "High performance" power plan makes the CPU run at full speed constantly, which generates heat even when you are just browsing. On Windows, go to Settings > System > Power & battery and set the power mode to "Balanced" or "Best power efficiency." On macOS, go to System Settings > Battery and enable Low Power Mode when you do not need full speed. The difference in everyday performance is barely noticeable, but the drop in heat and fan noise is immediate.

**6. Kill background apps and startup programs.** Every program running in the background uses CPU cycles, and every CPU cycle makes heat. Press Ctrl+Shift+Esc to open Task Manager, click the CPU column to sort, and close anything you do not recognize or need. Then go to the Startup apps tab and disable everything that is not essential — chat apps, updaters, game launchers. Fewer background processes mean a cooler, quieter laptop and longer battery life to boot.

**7. Repaste the thermal paste.** If your laptop is three or more years old and still overheats after cleaning, the thermal paste between the CPU/GPU and the heat pipe has probably dried out. This is the most effective fix for older machines, but it requires opening the laptop. You will need isopropyl alcohol, a lint-free cloth, and a tube of quality thermal paste (like Arctic MX-4). Remove the heat sink, wipe off the old gray paste, apply a rice-grain-sized dot of new paste, and reassemble. If you are not comfortable opening your laptop, any local repair shop can do this for a small fee. The temperature drop is often dramatic — 15 to 20 degrees is common.

**8. Check the battery and charging habits.** A swollen or failing battery generates excess heat and can even be dangerous. If your laptop's trackpad is pushed up or the bottom case is bulging, the battery is swelling — stop using it and get the battery replaced immediately. Even a healthy battery heats up while charging, so for long gaming or editing sessions, consider running the laptop plugged in with the battery capped at 80% (many modern laptops have a "battery care" or "conservation mode" in the manufacturer app that does exactly this). Less heat during charging means a longer-lasting battery and a cooler machine.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I know if my laptop is actually overheating?** Download a free tool like HWMonitor or Core Temp (Windows) or Intel Power Gadget (Mac) and check your CPU temperature under load. Idle temperatures of 40–55°C are normal; under heavy load, 80–90°C is typical, but if you are hitting 95°C+ constantly, you have a problem. Throttling (the CPU dropping its speed to cool down) is the real symptom to watch for.

**When should I replace the fan?** If you have cleaned and repasted and the laptop still overheats, listen to the fan. A fan that rattles, grinds, or does not spin at all needs replacement — fans are cheap (usually under $20) and easy to swap if you can open the case. A fan that spins fine but blows weak air is usually a sign of a clogged heat sink, not a dead fan.

**Is it safe to use a laptop while it is overheating?** Short term, yes — modern chips throttle to protect themselves, so you will not melt anything. But sustained high heat degrades the battery and the motherboard components over time, and a throttled laptop is slow and frustrating. Fix it sooner rather than later.

**Can a software update cause overheating?** Sometimes. A buggy Windows update or a runaway app can peg the CPU at 100%. If overheating started suddenly after an update, check Task Manager for the offending process, roll back the update, or wait for a patch. Always check software before assuming it is a hardware problem.
