Of all the numbers that make up your credit score, credit utilization is the one you can change the fastest — sometimes in a single billing cycle. It is also the one most people misunderstand, chasing advice like "keep it under 30%" without knowing what "it" is, when it is measured, or why one maxed-out card can hurt even when your overall spending looks modest. This guide explains utilization the way the scoring models actually see it, and gives you the exact moves that lower it before your next statement closes.

## What credit utilization actually is

Utilization is the percentage of your revolving credit limits that you are using, measured as balance divided by limit. If you owe $3,200 across cards with $10,000 of total limits, your overall utilization is 32%. Scoring models like FICO weigh it under "amounts owed," worth about 30% of your score — second only to payment history.

Two details matter more than the headline number. First, models look at both your **overall** utilization across all cards and the **highest utilization on any single card**. A borrower at 15% overall with one card at 95% will score worse than one at a flat 25% everywhere. Second, utilization is measured from the balance your issuer **reports**, which is usually the balance on your statement closing date — not the balance on the due date, and not what you owe right now. That timing quirk is the key to the fastest fix, below.

## How much of your limit should you use?

Forget the idea that 30% is a target. The data behind the scores is a slope, not a cliff: people with the highest scores tend to use under 10% of their limits, and lower is steadily better down to a small nonzero amount. Practical zones: **under 10%** is the excellent zone; **10–30%** is fine for everyday life; **30–50%** quietly costs you points; **above 50%** — especially maxed-out cards — reads as financial stress to the models and can cost 50 points or more.

One nuance: exactly 0% everywhere is not quite optimal either, because a card reporting a $0 balance can look dormant. The sweet spot many credit experts aim for is a tiny reported balance — 1% to 3% — on one card, paying in full each month. You earn the "active, low use" signal without paying a cent of interest.

## The statement-date trick that changes your score in one cycle

Because issuers report the statement balance, *when* you pay matters as much as how much. Pay your card down a few days **before the statement closing date**, and the low balance is what gets reported — even if you charge the card right back up afterward. Pay the same amount on the due date instead, and last month's high balance is already on your report for the next 30 days.

This is why people who "pay in full every month" sometimes still show 40% utilization: they pay the full statement balance on the due date, but the next statement's spending has already been reported. If you are about to apply for a mortgage, auto loan, or apartment, make a mid-cycle payment to pull reported balances down, then let one statement close before you apply. Our [PMI guide](/posts/how-to-remove-pmi-and-when-you-can-drop-it/) and [house affordability guide](/posts/how-much-house-can-i-afford-on-a-us-salary/) both depend on this — a better score at application time means better pricing on the biggest loan of your life.

## Lowering utilization without paying anything off

Paying balances down is the main lever, but three others work too. **Ask for a limit increase**: the same $1,500 balance is 75% of a $2,000 limit but 30% of a $5,000 limit. Many issuers approve increases online with a soft inquiry — just do not spend the new room. **Open a new card cautiously**: it adds limit and dilutes utilization, but the hard inquiry and lower average account age cost a few points short-term, so it is a medium-term play, not a pre-mortgage move. **Spread spending across cards**: if one card is at 90% and another sits empty, moving everyday spend to the empty card lowers your worst single-card ratio even before you pay anything down.

What does *not* work: closing old cards (it shrinks your total limits and raises utilization overnight), carrying a balance "to build credit" (interest paid buys zero extra points), and debit-card spending (it never reports). Utilization also has almost no memory in most current scores — a terrible month stops hurting as soon as a low balance reports, which is genuinely good news if you are rebuilding.

## A simple monthly routine

Once a month, glance at each card's balance and limit and run the pair through the [utilization calculator](/tools/credit-utilization-calculator/). If any card is above 30%, schedule a payment to land before its closing date. If your overall ratio is above 10% and a big application is coming up in the next two months, pay the most-utilized card down first — per-card numbers matter. That five-minute habit protects the second-biggest factor in your score more reliably than any monitoring app notification.
