Nobody ever lost a ranking *because* their URL was 120 characters long. But long, messy URLs quietly cost you clicks, shares, and trust every single day — and unlike most SEO factors, URL length is entirely within your control.

This guide covers what actually matters about URL length in 2026, the practical limits, and how to write slugs that humans and search engines both like.

## What Google actually says about URL length

Google's guidance has been consistent for years: keep URLs **simple, descriptive, and reasonably short**. There is no official character limit for ranking, and Google can crawl very long URLs fine. But:

- **Google truncates long URLs in search results**, showing an ellipsis — the descriptive part you carefully wrote may never be seen.
- **Very long URLs (100+ characters)** correlate with lower click-through rates in multiple industry studies.
- **Excessive parameters** (`?id=48291&session=xyz&ref=...`) can cause crawl and duplicate-content headaches.

The takeaway: length is not a ranking factor, but it is a **click and usability factor** — which ultimately moves the same traffic needle.

## The practical limits (2026)

| Limit | Value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Ideal slug length | Under 60–75 characters | Fully visible in SERPs, easy to share |
| Comfortable max | ~100 characters | Still usable; start trimming beyond this |
| Browser address bar | ~2,000 characters | Technical ceiling, not a target |
| Social sharing | Shorter is better | Long URLs get mangled in posts and messages |

## Writing SEO-friendly slugs (7 rules)

1. **Use the primary keyword once.** `example.com/how-to-compress-pdf/` beats `example.com/page?id=123` and beats keyword-stuffed monstrosities.
2. **Keep it to 3–5 words.** Enough to describe the page, short enough to read at a glance.
3. **Use hyphens, never underscores.** Google treats hyphens as word separators; underscores join words into one token.
4. **Lowercase everything.** Mixed case creates duplicate-URL risk on case-sensitive servers.
5. **Drop stop words** (and, the, of) unless removing them makes the slug confusing.
6. **No dates in evergreen URLs.** `/best-cameras-2026/` needs updating yearly; `/best-cameras/` stays fresh with a content refresh.
7. **Never change a published URL** without a 301 redirect. The SEO cost of a URL change dwarfs any benefit of a prettier slug.

## URL structure beyond the slug

Length is one piece; structure is the other:

- **Shallow beats deep.** `site.com/guide/` outranks `site.com/blog/2026/10/category/guide/` in usability (and slightly in crawl efficiency). Every folder level is friction.
- **HTTPS is mandatory.** Non-HTTPS URLs trigger browser warnings that kill clicks regardless of length.
- **Avoid parameters for content pages.** `?page=2` for pagination is fine; `?id=48291` as your only identifier is not. Use descriptive paths.
- **Trailing slash consistency.** Pick one (with or without) and canonicalize — mixed versions split signals.

## Fixing long URLs you already have

Already published with ugly URLs? The decision tree:

- **Low-traffic page, few backlinks** → change the URL and 301-redirect the old one. Cheap and safe.
- **High-traffic page with backlinks** → leave the URL alone. The redirect risk outweighs the cosmetic gain. Optimize the title and description instead.
- **Parameter hell** (`?utm_source=...` indexed) → canonical tags pointing to the clean URL, and stop linking the parameterized versions internally.

A 301 redirect passes most ranking signals, but "most" is not "all" — and every redirect adds a small crawl cost. Change URLs deliberately, not casually.

## Good URLs vs bad URLs: side by side

Nothing teaches like examples:

**Good:**
- `dollarwise.xyz/tools/pdf-compressor/` — short, descriptive, keyword-rich
- `example.com/how-to-tune-guitar/` — reads like the headline, memorable
- `example.com/uk-income-tax-calculator/` — tells you exactly what the page is

**Bad:**
- `example.com/page?id=48291&session=ab12cd` — meaningless, unshareable, untrustworthy
- `example.com/blog/2026/10/15/category/subcategory/finally-the-actual-article-title-here` — six levels deep before the content
- `example.com/Best_DEALS!!!/cheap-shoes-discount-buy-now` — uppercase, underscores, keyword stuffing, and punctuation that breaks in emails

The test: read the URL aloud to someone. If they can type it from memory and guess the page content, it is a good URL. If you have to spell out query parameters, it is not.

Finally, remember that URLs are forever-ish: once a URL earns backlinks and history, changing it costs ranking signals even with a perfect redirect. Get the slug right on day one, and you will never have to think about it again.

## A 5-minute URL audit routine

Once a quarter, run this quick audit on your own site: export your URLs from Search Console or a crawler, sort by length, and review anything over 100 characters. Fix the easy wins first — parameter-heavy URLs that should be rewritten, deep folder paths that can be flattened, and slugs stuffed with stop words. Leave high-traffic legacy URLs alone unless they are genuinely broken; the redirect risk is not worth cosmetic gains. Fifteen minutes per quarter keeps URL bloat from silently accumulating as your site grows.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a URL be for SEO?
Keep URLs under about 60–75 characters when you can. Short, descriptive URLs get more clicks and are easier to share; there is no hard penalty for longer URLs, but usability drops as they grow.

### Does URL length affect Google rankings?
Length itself is a very minor factor at most. The real SEO impact is indirect: shorter URLs earn more clicks, get shared more, and are easier to remember — all of which help performance.

### Should keywords be in the URL?
Yes, one or two descriptive keywords in the slug help users understand the page before clicking and give a tiny relevance signal. But never stuff keywords — readability first.
