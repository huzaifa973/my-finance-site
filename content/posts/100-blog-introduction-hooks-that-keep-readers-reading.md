Most readers decide whether to stay on a page within seconds, and they decide based on the introduction. A slow, generic opening — a definition, a throat-clearing sentence, a vague promise — sends them straight back to the search results. A strong hook does the opposite: it creates curiosity, names a real problem, or tells a short story that makes the reader need the next paragraph. Good introductions are not luck; they follow patterns, and patterns can be practiced.
This pack gives you 100 introduction hooks across five styles: curiosity openers, story leads, mistake warnings, bold promises, and direct questions. Each one is a complete opening line with a [topic] slot, so you can drop it at the top of any post and keep writing. Bloggers, newsletter writers, and content teams can use them to beat the blank page and cut bounce rates. Ten samples are below; the free download holds all 100.

## What's inside this hook pack

Curiosity hooks open a gap the reader wants closed — a surprising claim, a counterintuitive fact, or a secret the article will reveal. Story hooks start in the middle of a real moment, which builds instant trust because the writer has clearly lived the topic. Mistake hooks name the error readers are probably making right now, which makes the article feel personally relevant. Promise hooks state the outcome plainly and set a clear expectation. Question hooks mirror the reader's own doubt back at them, which stops the scroll.
The download groups all 100 hooks by style, so you can pick the style that fits the post: curiosity for surprising topics, story for personal experience, mistake hooks for tutorials, promises for how-tos, and questions for opinion pieces. Rotate styles across your site so no two consecutive posts open the same way.

## How to use this resource

1. Pick a hook style that fits the post: story for personal experience, mistake hooks for tutorials, curiosity for surprising findings.
2. Replace [topic] with your real subject and adjust the wording so it sounds like you, not like a template.
3. Follow the hook immediately with a concrete promise: what the reader will learn, and why it matters to them.
4. Read the first three sentences aloud. If they sound like every other blog post, try a different hook from the pack.
5. Track which styles keep readers longest using your analytics, then lean on the winners for your most important posts.

A hook is a promise — always deliver the answer or story it implies, or readers will stop trusting your openings.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a blog introduction be?**

Two to four short paragraphs is plenty. Say what the post covers, why it matters, and what the reader will get — then get to the content. Long introductions are the most common reason readers leave.

**Can I use the same hook on multiple posts?**

You can reuse a style, but write a fresh line each time. Two posts opening with the identical sentence look templated and hurt trust, even if the rest of the article is original.

**Do hooks really affect search rankings?**

Indirectly, yes. A strong introduction keeps readers on the page longer and reduces bounces back to search results — both signals that search engines notice over time.
