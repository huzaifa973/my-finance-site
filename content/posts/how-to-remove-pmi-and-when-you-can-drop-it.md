Private mortgage insurance is the most annoying line in a new homeowner's budget: a monthly premium, often $100 to $200, that protects your lender if you stop paying — and does absolutely nothing for you. If you bought your home with less than 20% down on a conventional loan, you are almost certainly paying it. The good news is that PMI is not forever, and in many cases you can end it months or even years earlier than the default schedule if you know the rules. This guide explains exactly when PMI can come off, the three routes to removing it, and how to ask your loan servicer the right way so the answer is yes.

## What PMI is and why you are paying it

When a buyer puts down less than 20%, lenders see a thinner cushion between the loan balance and the home's value, which means more risk if prices dip or the borrower defaults early. Private mortgage insurance transfers part of that risk to an insurance company, and the borrower pays the premium. Rates typically run from about 0.3% to 1.5% of the loan amount per year depending on your credit score and down payment, divided into monthly payments. On a $380,000 loan, even a mid-range premium is roughly $145 a month — money that builds no equity and buys you nothing you can use.

It helps to know which kind of mortgage insurance you have, because the removal rules differ completely. Conventional loans carry PMI, which the Homeowners Protection Act lets you remove. FHA loans carry a mortgage insurance premium (MIP) instead, and for most FHA borrowers who put down less than 10%, MIP lasts for the entire life of the loan — the only exit is refinancing into a conventional loan once you have enough equity. Everything below is about conventional PMI.

## The three ways PMI comes off

### 1. Automatic termination at 78%

Federal law requires your servicer to automatically terminate PMI when your loan balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the home's original value, based on the original amortization schedule, as long as you are current on your payments. You do not have to ask — but "automatic" deserves a watchful eye. Servicers make mistakes, and the date is based on the original schedule, not on any extra payments you have made. Mark the month on your calendar and verify the charge disappears from your statement.

### 2. Borrower-requested removal at 80%

You do not have to wait for 78%. Once your balance reaches 80% of the original value, you can request cancellation in writing. Servicers can require that you have a good payment history, that you certify no subordinate liens (like a second mortgage or HELOC) exist on the property, and sometimes that you pay for an appraisal to confirm the value has not declined. Our free [PMI removal calculator](/tools/pmi-removal-calculator/) shows you the exact balance you need to reach and the month you will get there at your current pace — and how much sooner an extra monthly payment gets you there.

### 3. Removal based on your home's current value

If your home has appreciated, you may not need to pay the balance down to 80% of the *original* value at all. Many servicers will accept a new appraisal showing you are at 80% loan-to-value against the current value. Investor rules usually require some seasoning first — commonly two years of payments if the value jump comes from appreciation alone, or as little as a few months if you made substantial documented improvements. The appraisal typically costs $300 to $500, which sounds steep until you compare it with another year of PMI premiums. If removing PMI a year early saves $1,700 in premiums for a $400 appraisal, the math is obvious.

## How to request PMI removal, step by step

**Step 1: Find your numbers.** You need your original home value (the purchase price or original appraised value, whichever was used), your current balance, and your monthly PMI premium. All three are on your closing documents and monthly statement.

**Step 2: Run the math.** Use the [PMI removal calculator](/tools/pmi-removal-calculator/) to see your current loan-to-value percentage and the balance you need. If you are close to 80%, a single extra principal payment might cross the line this month instead of next year.

**Step 3: Write to your servicer.** Send a written request — servicers are required to act on written requests, and a paper trail protects you. State that your balance has reached (or will reach) 80% of the original value, that your payments are current, and ask them to confirm cancellation and list any conditions, such as an appraisal.

**Step 4: Complete any appraisal promptly.** If they order a valuation, schedule it fast; delays push removal into another month of premiums.

**Step 5: Verify.** Check the next two statements to confirm the PMI charge is gone, and keep the confirmation letter with your mortgage paperwork.

## How to reach 80% faster

Every extra dollar of principal shortens the PMI timeline, but some moves work far better than others. Biweekly payments — half a payment every two weeks — add up to one full extra payment a year without a noticeable monthly strain. A tax refund or bonus applied directly to principal can jump you across the line in one stroke. And if rates have dropped meaningfully since you bought, refinancing may remove PMI and lower your rate at once — just run the closing costs against the savings: refinancing to drop PMI only pays if the numbers clear the break-even point, in practice, compare the refinance's total closing costs against the total PMI and interest you would otherwise pay, using a break-even calculation.

One caution: do not drain your emergency fund to hit 80%. PMI is annoying; being house-rich and cash-poor when the water heater dies is dangerous. Keep three months of expenses liquid first, then attack the balance.
