Choosing colors is the design decision everyone feels and few can explain. A website with the wrong palette looks "off" in half a second — visitors cannot name why, but they trust it less and leave sooner. The good news is that good palettes are not taste or talent; they are structure. Designers use a handful of proven formulas built on the color wheel, a strict 60-30-10 balance, and honest contrast checks. This guide walks through the whole process, from one starting color to a palette your whole site or brand can live on.

## Start with one color that means something

Every palette begins with a single anchor color, and the anchor should come from meaning, not from scrolling swatches. Colors carry associations your visitors absorb instantly: blues signal trust and stability (banks, health, tech), greens suggest growth, money, and nature, warm oranges and reds create energy and urgency, purples read creative and premium, and neutrals — charcoal, warm gray, off-white — give everything else room to breathe.

Start from what you already have. A logo color, a product's packaging, or the dominant tone in your best photograph can all anchor a palette. If you have a photo that captures your brand's mood, run it through a [color palette generator](/tools/color-palette-generator/) and pull its dominant colors — a palette extracted from real imagery automatically harmonizes with that imagery on your site.

## Build the palette with a harmony formula

Designers do not pick five colors independently; they pick relationships. The color wheel gives you four formulas that reliably work:

**Complementary** — two colors opposite each other (blue and orange). Maximum contrast and energy; ideal when one color must pop, like a call-to-action button. Soften it by using one color generously and the other sparingly.

**Analogous** — neighbors on the wheel (blue, teal, green). Calm, cohesive, low-risk. The safest choice for content sites and blogs; add contrast through lightness rather than hue.

**Triadic** — three colors evenly spaced (red, blue, yellow is the pure form). Balanced but vibrant; usually best with one dominant color and two accents at lower saturation.

**Monochromatic** — one hue at several lightness levels. Elegant and almost impossible to break; perfect for minimalist brands, though it can feel flat without strong typography.

Most generator tools, including our [color palette generator](/tools/color-palette-generator/), offer a harmony mode: pick your anchor, choose the formula, and adjust from there. What you do next matters more than the formula itself.

## Apply the 60-30-10 rule

A palette is not a democracy. Roughly **60%** of your design should be the dominant color — usually a light neutral background or your calmest hue. **30%** goes to a secondary color for headers, sections, cards, and navigation. **10%** is the accent: buttons, links, prices, badges — the elements you actually want clicked. When a design feels chaotic, it is almost never the colors themselves; it is five colors fighting at equal volume. This ratio is also why a strong accent works: an orange button on a blue-and-white page gets clicked precisely because orange only exists there.

Write your final palette down as HEX codes in one place — a brand sheet, a CSS variables block, a note pinned in your design tool. Consistency is what turns "some colors" into a brand: the same five codes, everywhere, every time. If you are extracting colors from an image, click any swatch in the [palette generator](/tools/color-palette-generator/) to copy its HEX exactly.

## Check contrast before you fall in love

A beautiful palette that fails contrast is a broken palette. Body text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background (the WCAG AA standard); large headings need 3:1. The most common failure is light gray text on white — elegant in a mockup, unreadable in sunlight — followed by white text on yellow or pastel buttons. Also design for the roughly 1 in 12 men with color-vision deficiency: never let color be the *only* signal (a red error border should come with an icon or text), and avoid red-green pairings for charts that must be compared.

## A worked example: from one photo to a full site palette

Say you run a coffee subscription brand. Your hero photo is warm: deep brown beans, cream ceramics, a terracotta cup. Extracting its colors gives roughly a dark roast brown (#3E2723), a warm tan (#A1887F), cream (#EFEBE9), terracotta (#BF5B34), and a muted sage (#8A9A5B) from a plant in frame. Assign by ratio: cream as the 60% background, brown for text and header (30% shared with tan for cards and section bands), terracotta as the 10% accent on every buy button, sage reserved for badges and freshness callouts. Five colors from one photo, every role assigned — and the site automatically matches the photography, which is the hardest part of palette design done for you.

Test the result on a real page, not a swatch sheet: headings, a paragraph, a button, a form. Palettes only prove themselves in context. Then lock it. The temptation to keep tweaking is strong, but a good palette used consistently for a year will outperform a perfect palette that changes monthly, because recognition is the entire point.
