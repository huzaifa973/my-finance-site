The average American household spends over $3,000 a year eating out — and that's the *average*, dragged down by people who rarely do. If you buy lunch most workdays and order dinner twice a week, you're easily spending $500–$700 a month on food you didn't cook.

Meal prepping flips that math. Cooking in batches brings your per-meal cost to $2–$4, and two focused hours on Sunday can cover most of your week's eating. The realistic savings: **about $300 a month, or $3,600 a year** — a vacation, an emergency fund, or a maxed IRA contribution, funded entirely by leftovers.

Here's the complete system, built for people who don't love cooking.

## The $60/week grocery framework

Forget complicated meal plans. Shop in five cheap categories and you'll always have meals:

1. **Grains & starches ($8):** rice, oats, pasta, tortillas, potatoes. The calorie base of every cheap meal.
2. **Proteins ($20):** chicken thighs (not breasts — cheaper and tastier), eggs, dried beans/lentils, canned tuna, peanut butter. Buy what's on sale and build around it.
3. **Frozen vegetables ($8):** broccoli, stir-fry blends, peas, spinach. As nutritious as fresh, zero spoilage, often $1–$1.25/bag.
4. **Fresh produce ($12):** bananas, apples, carrots, cabbage, onions, seasonal fruit. Shop the store's loss leaders and whatever's in season.
5. **Flavor ($12):** soy sauce, hot sauce, garlic, spices, oil, broth. This category is what makes cheap food taste like *food* instead of punishment.

**Golden rule: shop your pantry first.** Plan meals around what you already own, then buy only the gaps. Most wasted grocery money is duplicate buying.

## The 2-hour Sunday system

Meal prep fails when it's ambitious. This version is deliberately boring — and it works:

**Hour 1 — Cook the big batches (all at once):**
- Start rice or grains in a rice cooker or pot (set and forget)
- Sheet pan of chicken thighs + chopped vegetables in the oven (425°F, 25 min)
- Pot of beans/lentils or chili simmering on the stove
- Hard-boil 6–8 eggs

**Hour 2 — Assemble and store:**
- Divide into containers: grain + protein + veg = a complete meal
- Portion snacks: cut carrots, portion nuts, wash fruit
- Label everything with the date (masking tape + marker)
- Fridge 3–4 days' worth; freeze the rest in flat zip bags (they thaw fast and stack)

Total active work: about 40 minutes. The rest is waiting while you do laundry.

## 5 batch recipes under $1.50/serving

These are forgiving, scalable, and survive reheating — the three laws of meal prep:

1. **Chicken burrito bowls** — rice, black beans, chicken thighs, corn, salsa. ~$1.40/serving. Add fresh toppings (avocado, lime) at serving time, not before storing.
2. **Lentil and vegetable soup** — red lentils, carrots, onion, garlic, broth, cumin. ~$0.80/serving. Freezes beautifully; tastes better on day two.
3. **Sheet-pan sausage and veg** — kielbasa or Italian sausage, potatoes, peppers, onion, olive oil. ~$1.50/serving. One pan, zero skill required.
4. **Overnight oats (breakfast)** — oats, milk, peanut butter, banana, chia seeds. ~$0.90/serving. Five jars in five minutes, grab-and-go all week.
5. **Egg fried rice** — day-old rice, eggs, frozen peas/carrots, soy sauce, sesame oil. ~$1.10/serving. Faster than delivery, every time.

Rotate 2–3 of these weekly and you'll never get bored enough to quit.

## Storage rules that prevent food waste

Wasted prepped food is wasted money. Follow the 3-4-3 rule:

- **3–4 days** in the fridge for cooked meat, grains, and most meals
- **3 months** in the freezer for soups, stews, and grain bowls
- **Keep components separate** when textures matter: dressings, crispy toppings, and fresh herbs get added at serving time

Cool food before sealing (30–45 minutes on the counter, no more than 2 hours), and reheat to steaming throughout. If something smells off or you're unsure — throw it out. A $2 container isn't worth food poisoning.

## Eating out without blowing it up

Meal prep doesn't mean never eating out — it means eating out *on purpose*. The system that works:

- **The 90/10 rule:** ~90% of meals prepped, ~10% (3–4 meals/week) flexible for social eating and cravings.
- **A dining-out line in your budget:** Give it a fixed number ($80–$120/month for most budgets). When it's gone, it's gone — no guilt, no overspend.
- **Prep the expensive meals first:** Restaurant lunches are the worst value in food. Prepping weekday lunches alone saves ~$200/month for most office workers.
- **Keep a "lazy backup":** Frozen dumplings, rotisserie chicken, or eggs + toast for nights you'd otherwise order delivery. A $4 backup beats a $28 DoorDash order.

### FAQ

**Q:** How much money does meal prepping actually save?

A: Most households save $200–$400/month. A homemade meal costs $2–$4 per serving versus $12–$18 for takeout or fast-casual. Prepping just weekday lunches alone (20 meals) saves roughly $200/month over buying lunch out.

**Q:** How long does meal-prepped food last in the fridge?

A: Most cooked meals stay good 3–4 days refrigerated. For a full week of prep, refrigerate 3–4 days' worth and freeze the rest, then thaw overnight. Soups, stews, and grain bowls freeze best; salads and crispy items should be prepped fresh.

**Q:** What are the cheapest foods to meal prep?

A: Rice, oats, dried beans and lentils, eggs, chicken thighs, frozen vegetables, bananas, peanut butter, and seasonal produce. These staples cost under $1–$2 per pound and form the base of dozens of meals.

**Q:** Do I need special containers for meal prep?

A: No. Any airtight containers work to start — even reused jars. If buying, get glass containers with locking lids (microwave- and dishwasher-safe, last years) in one uniform size so they stack neatly in the fridge.
