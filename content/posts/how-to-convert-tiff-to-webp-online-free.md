TIFF is the format of archives, scanners, and professional photography — and it is brutally heavy. A single high-resolution TIFF scan can weigh 50 MB or more, which makes it useless for websites, email, and sharing. WebP is the modern answer: dramatically smaller files with quality your eyes cannot tell apart from the original. Here is how to convert TIFF to WebP free, what settings to use, and when to choose lossless versus lossy.

## Why TIFF files are so huge

TIFF (Tagged Image File Format) was designed in the 1980s for print and archiving, where fidelity mattered more than file size. A TIFF typically stores every pixel with little or no compression:

- A 24-megapixel photo as TIFF: **60–70 MB**
- The same photo as high-quality WebP: **2–4 MB**
- A 300 DPI letter-size color scan as TIFF: **25 MB**
- The same scan as WebP: **1–2 MB**

That 90%+ size reduction is why nobody puts raw TIFFs on the web. Browsers can technically display TIFFs, but loading a page of them would take forever — and many platforms reject TIFF uploads outright.

## WebP in 30 seconds

WebP, developed by Google, is now supported by every modern browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari 14+, and mobile browsers). It offers two modes:

- **Lossy WebP:** like a smarter JPEG — typically 25–35% smaller than JPEG at the same visual quality.
- **Lossless WebP:** like a smarter PNG — typically 20–30% smaller than PNG, with bit-perfect reproduction.

For TIFF conversion, this means one format handles both photographic scans (lossy) and line art, text documents, or graphics (lossless).

## Step-by-step: convert TIFF to WebP online

1. **Gather your TIFF files.** Phone scans, flatbed scans, camera exports — any standard TIFF works, including multi-page TIFFs (each page converts separately).
2. **Upload them to the converter.** Drag and drop, single or batch. Try the free tool: /tools/tiff-to-webp-converter/ — conversion runs entirely in your browser, so your scans never upload to a server.
3. **Choose lossy or lossless.**
   - *Photos, artwork, general scans:* lossy at quality 85–90. Visually identical to the TIFF, tiny file.
   - *Text documents, line art, images you will edit further:* lossless. Bit-perfect, still far smaller than the TIFF.
4. **Convert and download.** Each TIFF becomes a .webp file. Compare a converted file side-by-side with the original at 100% zoom to confirm the quality.
5. **Spot-check text legibility.** For document scans, zoom into small text — if it is crisp, your settings are right.

## Recommended settings by use case

| Use case | Mode | Quality | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Website photos | Lossy | 80–85 | Best size/quality balance for the web |
| Photo archiving | Lossless | — | Bit-perfect preservation, still much smaller than TIFF |
| Scanned documents | Lossy | 90 | Keeps small text sharp |
| Images for print layout | Lossless | — | No generational loss if re-exported later |
| Email attachments | Lossy | 75–80 | Smallest files that still look clean |

When in doubt, start at quality 85 lossy — it is the sweet spot where virtually nobody can see a difference from the original.

## TIFF to WebP vs. TIFF to JPEG

JPEG is the familiar choice, but WebP wins on every axis that matters here:

- **File size:** WebP lossy is roughly 25–35% smaller than JPEG at equal visual quality.
- **Transparency:** TIFFs with alpha channels convert cleanly to WebP; JPEG cannot do transparency at all.
- **Lossless option:** JPEG has no lossless mode — WebP does, replacing both JPEG *and* PNG in one format.
- **Compatibility:** the one historical knock against WebP (Safari support) ended years ago; all current browsers render it.

The only reason to pick JPEG over WebP today is a specific platform that still rejects WebP uploads — increasingly rare.

## Batch-converting a whole archive

Digitized photo collections and scan backlogs often mean hundreds of TIFFs. A few tips for bulk jobs:

- **Convert in batches of 20–50** rather than all at once — browser-based tools process locally, and huge batches can strain memory.
- **Keep the originals.** Store the TIFFs on an external drive or cloud backup before converting; WebP is your working/sharing copy, TIFF remains the archival master.
- **Preserve filenames.** A good converter keeps original filenames with the new .webp extension so your organization survives the conversion.
- **Check a sample first.** Convert 3–5 representative files, inspect them closely, then run the full batch with the same settings.

## Common problems and fixes

- **"The WebP looks blurry."** Quality was set too low — reconvert at 85+. Below 70, lossy WebP shows visible artifacts on detailed images.
- **Text in scans looks fuzzy.** Switch to lossless for that file, or raise lossy quality to 90–95.
- **A platform rejects the .webp file.** Some older uploaders still want JPEG/PNG — convert that specific file to the required format as a one-off.
- **Multi-page TIFF only converted one page.** Each page becomes its own WebP; check the download list for all pages before closing the tab.
- **Colors look slightly off.** TIFFs with unusual color profiles (CMYK from print workflows) should be converted to sRGB first for accurate web display.

TIFF to WebP is one of the highest-leverage conversions in digital housekeeping: the same images, a tenth of the weight, ready for the modern web. Convert once, keep the TIFF masters backed up, and never email a 50 MB scan again.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why convert TIFF to WebP?**
TIFF files are enormous — a single scan can be 50+ MB. WebP delivers visually identical quality at a fraction of the size (often 90%+ smaller), loads fast on the web, and is supported by all modern browsers.

**Does converting TIFF to WebP lose quality?**
It can be completely lossless if you choose lossless WebP. Lossy WebP at high quality (85–95) is visually indistinguishable from the TIFF for photos while shrinking files dramatically.

**Can I convert multiple TIFF files to WebP at once?**
Yes — batch conversion processes a whole folder of TIFFs in one pass, which is ideal for digitized photo archives and scanned document sets.

**Is WebP better than JPEG for converted TIFFs?**
Usually yes. WebP's lossy mode beats JPEG on quality-per-byte for photos, and its lossless mode beats PNG for graphics — so one format covers both jobs a TIFF might have been doing.
