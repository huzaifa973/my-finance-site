Thumbnail text has one job: make a scroller stop. It is not a title, not a summary, and definitely not a paragraph — it is three to six words that create a gap the viewer wants closed. These fifty templates cover the angles that consistently stop thumbs: curiosity, results, mistakes, comparisons, and bold claims, each short enough to read at a glance on a phone screen. Pair them with a clear face or object image, and keep the words to one short line.

## What's inside this resource

The templates are grouped by angle so you can match the text to the video's real hook — a curiosity line on a tutorial feels wrong, and a results line on an opinion piece feels empty. Swap the bracketed words for your topic, keep the word count low, and test: thumbnails are the highest-leverage test on any video.

## How to use this resource

1. Match the text angle to the video's real hook — curiosity for discoveries, results for transformations.
2. Keep thumbnail text to six words or fewer; test readability at phone size.
3. Use high-contrast colours and a font that survives compression.
4. Never put the same words in the thumbnail and the title — let them work together, not repeat.
5. Test two thumbnails per important video and keep the winner.

A resource is only useful when it gets used — pick one item today, use it as written, and adapt it from experience rather than from theory.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many words should thumbnail text have?**

Three to six. Viewers decide in under a second, and small text is unreadable on phones — if it needs a sentence, it belongs in the title, not the thumbnail.

**Should thumbnail text repeat the title?**

No. The title and thumbnail are a team: the image and short text create curiosity, the title adds context. Repeating wastes half your space.

**Do clickbait-style lines hurt the channel?**

Misleading lines hurt: viewers click, feel tricked, and leave fast, which damages retention. Bold lines are fine when the video delivers on them.
