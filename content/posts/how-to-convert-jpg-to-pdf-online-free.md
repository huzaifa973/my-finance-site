A rental application wants your documents as one PDF — but all you have are phone photos. A client asks for your portfolio as a single file, not twelve separate images. A visa portal rejects JPG uploads outright. Converting JPG to PDF is one of the most common document chores in the world, and it takes under a minute once you know the right method.

Here's how to convert JPG to PDF free, on any device.

## Why convert images to PDF?

- **Official forms and applications:** job portals, university admissions, visa applications, and rental agencies almost universally require PDF. JPGs get rejected.
- **Single-file receipts and invoices:** a month of photographed receipts becomes one tidy PDF for your accountant or expense report.
- **Portfolios and lookbooks:** designers, photographers, and freelancers send one PDF instead of a scattered zip of images.
- **Archiving:** a PDF keeps pages in fixed order with consistent layout — no worrying about an image viewer shuffling your pages.
- **Signing:** PDFs support digital signatures and annotations; JPGs don't.

## Method 2: On your phone

- **iPhone/iPad:** open the Photos app, select the images, tap Share → **Print**. On the print preview, pinch outward on the thumbnail with two fingers — it becomes a PDF. Tap Share again to save or send it. It works, but ordering many pages is fiddly.
- **Android:** open the images in Google Photos or your gallery, tap Share → **Print**, and choose **Save as PDF** as the printer. Page order follows your selection order.

Phone methods are handy for two or three pages. For longer documents, the browser tool above is far less painful.

## Method 3: Built-in options on Windows and Mac

- **Windows:** select your images in File Explorer, right-click → **Print**. Choose **Microsoft Print to PDF** as the printer, pick a layout, and print. It's quick but gives you little control over page size and quality.
- **Mac:** open the images in Preview (select them all and open together), then File → Print → PDF → **Save as PDF**. You can drag thumbnails in the sidebar to reorder pages first.

## Tips for crisp, professional PDFs

- **Order pages before converting:** put the title page first, then follow a logical sequence. Renaming files with numbers (01, 02, 03…) helps the tool sort them automatically.
- **Match page size to content:** A4 or Letter for documents and forms; **fit-to-image** for photos and artwork so nothing gets stretched or letterboxed.
- **Keep quality high for official documents:** use the highest quality setting for IDs, certificates, and contracts — a blurry ID scan gets rejected and wastes days.
- **Compress only when needed:** if a portal caps uploads at 2 MB, compress the *images* before converting, or choose a balanced-quality setting. Don't compress an already-converted PDF twice.
- **Check orientation:** phone photos sometimes carry rotation metadata that converters ignore. Rotate any sideways images before adding them.
- **One document, one purpose:** don't mix unrelated documents into one PDF. A landlord wants your pay slips and ID together; they don't want your medical records in the same file.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does converting JPG to PDF reduce image quality?**
Not if you choose high-quality settings — the images are embedded as-is. Quality drops only if the tool downscales or compresses them, so avoid 'small file' presets when crispness matters.

**Can I combine multiple JPG images into one PDF?**
Yes. Add all your images to the converter, drag them into page order, and they'll be merged into a single multi-page PDF.

**How do I password-protect a PDF made from JPGs?**
Browser-based converters don't add passwords. Create the PDF first, then open it in Adobe Acrobat or a PDF editor and apply password protection under File → Protect.

**Why is my JPG-to-PDF file so large?**
Phone photos are several megabytes each, and a PDF embeds them all. Ten 4 MB photos make a ~40 MB PDF. Resize or compress the images before converting if you need a smaller file.
