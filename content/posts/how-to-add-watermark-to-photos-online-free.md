## Why watermark your photos

A watermark does three jobs at once. First, it **deters theft**: most image thieves grab whatever is easiest, and a visible watermark makes your photo the harder target. Second, it **claims credit**: when your shot goes viral on social media, your name travels with it instead of getting lost. Third, it **builds your brand**: photographers, designers, real-estate agents, and small businesses all use consistent watermarks so viewers learn to recognize their work at a glance.

Watermarks also matter for proofs and previews. If you send clients un-watermarked full-resolution files before payment, you have no leverage. A tasteful watermark across a proof says "this is yours when the invoice is paid" without an awkward conversation.

## Watermarking tips that actually work

**Match the watermark to the background.** White text vanishes on a bright sky; black text disappears in shadows. Use a semi-transparent white or black with a subtle shadow or outline so it reads on any background. Many tools offer a "contrast" or outline option for exactly this reason.

**Less is more.** A giant opaque logo screaming across the middle of the frame protects the image but ruins it for legitimate viewers. Aim for a watermark that takes a thief real effort to remove but that a normal viewer barely notices — roughly 40% opacity is the sweet spot for most photos.

**Tile for maximum protection.** For high-value images like stock photos or paid proofs, a repeating tiled watermark across the whole image is nearly impossible to clone out cleanly. Reserve this for proofs; it's too aggressive for portfolio pieces.

**Use your URL, not just your name.** "© Jane Doe" tells people who you are; "janedoe.com" tells them where to find (and hire) you. For business use, the website version pays for itself.

## Text vs logo watermarks: which to use

Text watermarks are fast, flexible, and readable at small sizes — perfect for social media posts, blog images, and everyday sharing. A simple "© YourName" in a clean font takes seconds and works on any photo.

Logo watermarks look more professional and reinforce brand identity, but they need a well-designed transparent PNG to work. If your logo has fine detail, it may become unreadable at watermark sizes — simplify it or pair it with text.

The practical answer: use text for volume and speed, and a logo for client-facing work where brand impression matters. Many photographers keep both presets saved and switch depending on the use case.

### Does watermarking reduce photo quality?
A watermark overlay itself doesn't reduce quality, but re-saving as JPEG adds a compression pass. Export as PNG or use the highest quality setting to keep the image sharp after watermarking. If you start from a RAW or TIFF master, watermark a copy and keep the original untouched.

### Where should I place a watermark on a photo?
Place it where it's hard to crop out — across a busy part of the image at low opacity — while keeping it subtle enough not to ruin the viewing experience. Tiled or center-bottom placement beats an easy-to-crop corner every time.
