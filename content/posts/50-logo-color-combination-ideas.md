Color is the first thing people notice about a logo, and it shapes how they feel about a brand before they read a single word. Navy suggests trust, gold suggests quality, coral suggests energy, and charcoal suggests seriousness — but the magic is in the combination, not any single color. A great pairing balances a dominant color with a supporting one, and a third accent used sparingly. Guessing at combinations wastes hours; starting from proven pairings does not.

## How to use this resource

1. Pick a combination that matches the feeling you want: navy and gold for trust, coral and charcoal for energy, teal and cream for calm.
2. Use the first color as your dominant color (about 60% of the design), the second as support (30%), and any third color as an accent (10%).
3. Test the combination on a dark background and a light background before committing — some pairings only work one way.
4. Check contrast: your logo must stay readable in a single color and at small sizes, not just in full color.
5. Write down the hex codes somewhere permanent. Brand colors drift when people guess from memory.

A palette is a decision, not a decoration — pick one, write it down, and use it everywhere.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many colors should a logo have?**

Two is ideal for most brands, three at most. More colors make logos harder to reproduce, more expensive to print, and harder for people to remember.

**Do hex codes matter, or can I just pick colors by eye?**

Hex codes matter. They guarantee the exact same color in every tool, on every screen, and with every designer. Picking by eye produces drift over time.

**Should my logo work in black and white?**

Yes. Logos appear in single-color situations constantly — stamps, invoices, embroidery, faxed forms. If it fails in black and white, the color combination is hiding a weak design.
