## Why portals insist on tiny PDFs

The 100KB limit is not arbitrary cruelty. Government portals, university systems, and job boards process millions of uploads, and small files solve real problems at that scale: they store cheaply, upload quickly on slow mobile connections, open instantly for reviewers, and keep databases from ballooning. A 100KB cap is also a quiet quality filter — it forces applicants to submit focused, single-purpose documents instead of 40-page portfolios.

Common places you will hit the limit: government job applications, visa and immigration document uploads, university admissions, scholarship forms, and exam registrations. Knowing the target before you scan saves a round trip.

## How PDF compression actually works

A PDF is a container, and most of its weight comes from images. Compression attacks the size in three ways:

- **Image recompression.** Photos and scans get re-encoded at lower quality or resolution. This is where 90% of the savings come from — a 300 DPI scan of a photo page can shrink tenfold at 150 DPI with barely visible change.
- **Downsampling.** Reducing image resolution from print-quality (300 DPI) to screen-quality (100-150 DPI) slashes bytes with minimal readability loss for text.
- **Structural optimization.** Removing duplicate fonts, unused objects, and redundant metadata trims the container itself.

Text itself compresses to almost nothing — a full page of text is a few kilobytes. If your PDF is huge, it is the images, not the words.

## Scan it right the first time

Most oversized PDFs start life as bad scans. If you control the scanning step, set the scanner or phone app to 150 DPI grayscale for text documents — that alone often produces a file already near the 100KB target with zero compression needed. Turn off "enhance" filters that add color backgrounds, crop tightly to the document edges instead of capturing your desk, and save directly as PDF rather than photographing to JPG and converting. A clean 150 DPI grayscale scan of a two-page form typically lands between 80KB and 120KB before you even touch a compressor.

## What survives 100KB — and what does not

Be realistic about the physics. What compresses well: 1-3 page text documents, forms, resumes, certificates, and ID scans. Text stays razor-sharp at any compression level, and simple graphics survive fine.

What struggles: 10+ page scanned bundles, documents full of color photographs, and pages with complex backgrounds or watermarks. A 100KB budget for a 20-page photo-heavy scan is simply not enough bytes — the math does not work, and the result will be mush.

For stubborn files, reduce before you compress: scan at 150 DPI instead of 300, choose grayscale for documents that do not need color, and split multi-part submissions into separate files if the portal allows it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I reduce a PDF to exactly 100KB?
Use a compressor that lets you set a target file size. It recompresses images and optimizes the file structure iteratively until the output lands at or under your target, instead of guessing with generic quality presets.

### Will compressing to 100KB make my PDF unreadable?
Not if the source is reasonable. Text stays perfectly readable; only photos get recompressed. A 10-page scanned document may struggle, but a 2-3 page form, resume, or certificate usually compresses cleanly.

### Why do upload portals demand PDFs under 100KB?
Small files store cheaply, upload fast on slow connections, open instantly for reviewers, and keep government and university databases manageable at massive scale. The cap also keeps submissions focused.

### What if my PDF won't go below 100KB?
Reduce the page count, split it into parts, or rescan at lower resolution. Extremely image-heavy documents have a hard compression floor — clean up the source (fewer pages, grayscale, 150 DPI) and try again.
