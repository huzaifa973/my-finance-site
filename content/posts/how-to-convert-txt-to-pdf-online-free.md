Plain text files are wonderfully simple — they open anywhere, weigh almost nothing, and never break. But when you need to share your writing with someone else, print it, or submit it formally, a .txt file feels unfinished. Text wraps oddly, there's no page structure, and formatting depends on whatever app opens it. Converting TXT to PDF solves all of that: your words land in a fixed, professional layout that looks identical on every device.

The good news is that the conversion is completely free and takes less than a minute. Here's how.

## Formatting tips for a professional result

Plain text has no bold, italics, or headings — but a few habits make converted PDFs look polished anyway.

**Structure with spacing.** Use blank lines between paragraphs and logical sections. Since .txt has no paragraph styles, consistent spacing is your main design tool.

**Mind your line length.** Very long lines wrap unpredictably. Keep lines under ~80 characters in your source text for predictable breaks, or let the converter's margins handle wrapping.

**Use simple heading markers.** Conventions like `CHAPTER 1` on its own line, or `=== Section Title ===`, survive conversion and scan well. You can restyle them after conversion if needed.

**Choose readable typography.** For body text, 11–12pt in a clean font (Georgia, Times New Roman, or a good sans-serif) with 1.3–1.5 line spacing is the sweet spot for print and screen.

**Watch special characters.** Curly quotes, em dashes, and symbols from word processors sometimes appear as odd glyphs in plain text. A quick find-and-replace for straight quotes and hyphens keeps the output clean.

## Batch conversion made simple

Need to convert a whole folder of .txt files? You have two practical paths. The fastest is to **combine first, convert once**: paste each file's content into one document with clear separators (like a centered `* * *` line) and convert the whole thing — perfect for chapters or sequential notes.

Alternatively, convert files **one at a time and merge the PDFs** afterward with any free PDF merger. This preserves individual documents as separate PDFs when you need them standalone, like a set of resumes or per-client letters.

## Privacy: why local conversion matters

Many online converters upload your file to a remote server, process it, and (hopefully) delete it. For a grocery list that's harmless; for a resume, contract, or unpublished manuscript, it's an unnecessary risk. A converter that runs **entirely in your browser** never transmits your text anywhere — the file goes from your device straight into a PDF on your device. For anything personal or work-related, that's the only kind of converter worth using.
