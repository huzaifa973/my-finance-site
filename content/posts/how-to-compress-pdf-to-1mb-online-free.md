## Why PDFs get so bloated

In almost every case, the answer is **images**. A single full-page color scan at 300 DPI can be 1–2MB; a 20-page scanned document easily hits 30MB. Photos embedded at print resolution, screenshots pasted at retina size, and scanned pages saved without compression are the usual suspects.

Secondary culprits: embedded fonts (each font family adds hundreds of KB), uncompressed vector graphics, and PDFs assembled from multiple sources with duplicated resources. But images account for 90%+ of oversized PDFs in practice — which is good news, because images are also the easiest thing to optimize.

## When compression isn't enough: smarter alternatives

**Split the PDF.** If a 40MB document must become sub-1MB files, split it into logical sections. Four 10MB chapters compress far more gracefully than one crushed 40MB file.

**Reduce page count.** Delete blank pages, duplicate scans, and unnecessary appendices. Every removed page is size you don't need to compress away.

**Re-scan at lower DPI.** For scanned documents, 150 DPI is plenty for on-screen reading and prints acceptably; 300 DPI doubles file size for quality nobody sees on a form upload. If you control the source, fix it there.

**Convert images before embedding.** If you're building the PDF yourself, downscale photos to the size they'll actually display (a photo shown at 3 inches wide doesn't need 4000 pixels) before creating the PDF. Prevention beats cure.

**Remove embedded extras.** Strip embedded fonts you don't need (standard fonts like Arial render fine without embedding), delete hidden layers, and flatten transparency. These trims can save hundreds of KB on complex documents.

**Downsample images selectively.** Not all images deserve equal quality: a full-page photo needs more resolution than a small logo. If your tool supports per-image settings, compress decorative images aggressively while keeping key figures and diagrams crisp.

**Watch for scanned color mode.** Color scans are 3x larger than grayscale and 24x larger than black-and-white. If the document is text-only, converting color scans to grayscale (or bitonal for clean text) before compression can cut size dramatically with zero readability loss.
