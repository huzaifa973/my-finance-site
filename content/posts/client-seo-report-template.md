A monthly SEO report is the difference between a client who renews and a client who quietly cancels. The work you do — technical fixes, content, links — is mostly invisible. Without a clear, regular report, the client only notices SEO when rankings drop. With one, they see progress, understand the plan, and trust the process even in slow months. The template below is the exact structure freelancers and agencies use: a plain-language executive summary for the busy client, hard numbers for the detail-oriented one, and a forward-looking plan that justifies next month's invoice.

Most SEO reports fail in one of two ways. They are either a raw data dump — twenty screenshots from analytics with no narrative — or a glossy "everything is great" brochure that falls apart the moment the client checks their own numbers. This template avoids both. It leads with an honest summary written for non-experts, then backs every claim with specific metrics: organic sessions, keyword rankings, conversions, technical health. There are sections for wins, challenges, and next steps, because a report that only celebrates wins reads like marketing. Clients pay for judgment, not just numbers, and this format puts your judgment front and center.

## What's inside this report template

The template opens with report metadata — client name, reporting period, and who prepared it — so nothing gets mixed up across clients and months. The executive summary is the heart: three to four sentences a non-technical client can read in thirty seconds and understand exactly where things stand. Then comes the KPI table with fill-in rows for organic sessions, keyword visibility, conversions, and technical health, each with this month, last month, and percent change columns so progress is instantly visible.

The keyword rankings section tracks your priority terms individually — position this month versus last, with room for notes on why a term moved. The traffic and engagement section captures where visitors come from and what they do: top landing pages, bounce or engagement rate, average position from Search Console. The actions completed section is where you document the actual work — technical fixes, content published, links earned — because this is what the client paid for and it's the first thing they forget. It closes with next month's plan and open items, so the client always knows what happens next. Fill the brackets, delete what doesn't apply, and send.

## How to use this template

1. Duplicate the template for each client and fill in the metadata — client name, website, and reporting period — so reports never get mixed up.
2. Pull your numbers first: organic sessions and conversions from analytics, positions and click-through rate from Search Console, ranking movement from your rank tracker.
3. Write the executive summary last, after you've seen all the data. Lead with the trend, name the biggest win, be honest about any challenge, and state what's next.
4. In the actions completed section, list everything you did — clients forget the invisible work, and this section is what justifies the invoice.
5. Send it in the first week of each month with a one-paragraph email: the summary, the top KPI change, and the link to the full report. Offer a 15-minute call for questions.

Consistency beats polish. A plain, honest report sent every month builds more trust than a beautiful one sent sporadically.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long should a client SEO report be?**

One to two pages. The executive summary plus the KPI table covers 90% of what clients read. The detailed tables exist for the few who want proof — most clients skim the summary and glance at the numbers.

**Should I include competitor data in the report?**

Only when it's useful context. A brief note like "competitor X launched a content hub in this topic" explains a ranking dip and shows you're watching the market. A full competitor analysis belongs in a separate strategy document, not the monthly report.

**What if the numbers are bad this month?**

Report them anyway, with context and a plan. "Traffic fell 12% after the site migration — expected, recovering, and here are the three fixes in progress" keeps a client. A missing or suspiciously vague report loses them.
