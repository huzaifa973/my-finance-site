Canada taxes you twice — once federally, once provincially — and the two layers stack. Understanding just the federal brackets is only half the picture, but it is the half that is the same for every Canadian, so it is the right place to start.

This guide covers the 2026 federal brackets, the basic personal amount, how provincial tax layers on top, and how to estimate your total bill.

## The 2026 federal tax brackets

Canada uses marginal brackets: each slice of income is taxed only at its own rate. Approximate 2026 federal brackets (indexed annually — verify with the CRA):

| Taxable income | Federal rate |
|---|---|
| Up to ~$58,523 | 14% |
| ~$58,523 – $117,045 | 20.5% |
| ~$117,045 – $181,440 | 26% |
| ~$181,440 – $258,482 | 29% |
| Over ~$258,482 | 33% |

A notable change: the lowest federal rate dropped from 15% to **14%** — a small but real cut worth a few hundred dollars for most earners.

**A worked example:** on $70,000 of taxable income, you pay 14% on the first ~$58,523 and 20.5% only on the remaining ~$11,477 — roughly $10,546 federal, *before* credits. Your effective federal rate is about 15%, nowhere near your 20.5% marginal bracket.

## The basic personal amount: your tax-free zone

Every Canadian resident gets the **Basic Personal Amount (BPA)** — about **$16,452** for 2026. It works as a non-refundable tax credit at the lowest rate, effectively making the first $16,452 of income tax-free federally.

Two things worth knowing:

- **It phases down for high earners** — above roughly $181,440 of net income, the enhanced amount shrinks toward a base of about $14,829.
- **Provinces have their own basic amounts** — Ontario's, BC's, Alberta's, and others' differ, and they stack with the federal one. Your true tax-free zone is federal + provincial combined.

## Provincial tax: the second layer

This is where Canadian tax gets personal. Each province and territory sets its own brackets and rates, and the spread is dramatic:

- **Alberta** has the lowest top provincial rate (10–15% range) with high thresholds.
- **Quebec** charges the highest top rates (up to ~25.75%) but administers its own tax system separately.
- **Ontario** starts at 5.05% on the first ~$53,891, stepping up through several brackets.
- **BC** starts at 5.06% with its own ladder.

Your province of residence **on December 31** determines which province taxes your entire year's income — a detail that genuinely matters if you move in December.

Combined federal + provincial marginal rates can reach **40–54%** for top earners depending on province. That is why "which province" is a real financial planning question in Canada, not just a lifestyle one.

## How to estimate your Canadian income tax (4 steps)

1. **Start with taxable income** — gross income minus RRSP contributions, union dues, and other deductions.
2. **Calculate federal tax** with [DollarWise's free Canada Income Tax Calculator](/tools/canada-income-tax-calculator/) using the 2026 federal brackets.

4. **Subtract your credits** — basic personal amount, Canada Employment Amount, medical expenses, tuition, and charitable donations all reduce the bill.

## Credits and deductions that shrink the bill

- **RRSP contributions** — reduce taxable income dollar for dollar (within your limit). The single most powerful lever for most earners.
- **TFSA** — no deduction going in, but growth and withdrawals are tax-free. Perfect complement to the RRSP.
- **Canada Employment Amount** — a credit for working income (around $1,501 federally for 2026).
- **Medical expenses and charitable donations** — often overlooked; donations above $200 earn a *higher* credit rate.
- **Splitting with a spouse** — pension income splitting and spousal RRSPs can move income to the lower-earning partner's brackets.

## Common Canadian tax mistakes

- **Confusing marginal and average rates.** Being "in the 26% bracket" does not mean you pay 26% overall — most of your income was taxed at lower rates.
- **Ignoring the December 31 rule.** Moving provinces late in the year changes your whole year's provincial tax.
- **Missing the RRSP deadline.** Contributions for a tax year must be made by early March of the following year — the calendar matters.
- **Not filing to "save" money.** Low earners often skip filing and forfeit GST/HST credits, the Canada Workers Benefit, and other refunds. Filing is how you *get* money back.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are Canada's federal tax brackets for 2026?
Approximately: 14% to $58,523; 20.5% to $117,045; 26% to $181,440; 29% to $258,482; and 33% above. These are indexed annually, so treat online figures as approximate and verify with the CRA.

### How much can I earn tax-free in Canada in 2026?
The federal basic personal amount is about $16,452 for 2026, meaning that much income is effectively tax-free at the federal level. Provinces have their own basic amounts on top.

### Do I pay federal and provincial tax separately?
Yes — they stack. You calculate federal tax on your income using federal brackets, then calculate provincial tax using your province's brackets, and pay the sum. Your province of residence on December 31 determines which provincial rates apply.

### Are online tax calculators accurate?
They are good estimates for planning, not official figures. Brackets are indexed yearly and credits vary by situation. Verify important numbers against CRA publications before filing or making big decisions.
