Every platform has a sweet spot: too big and your page crawls or your upload gets rejected, too small and your photo turns to mush. This cheat sheet gives you fifty concrete file size targets for the places your images actually go — websites, social feeds, email, messaging apps, and print. It is built for bloggers, small business owners, designers, and anyone who uploads photos regularly and wants a number to aim for instead of guessing. Keep it beside your export settings and you will stop the endless cycle of exporting, checking, and re-exporting the same image.

## What's inside this resource

The targets are grouped by use: website images first, since speed affects every visitor, then social media, email and messaging, and finally print and archive formats. Each entry names the use, a recommended format, a target size, and a pixel dimension, so you can set your export preset once and trust it. The numbers favor fast loading without visible quality loss — a hero image under 200 KB, a blog image under 150 KB, and social uploads sized to each platform's display size rather than your camera's full resolution.

## How to use this resource

1. Find the row that matches where your image will appear and note its format, target size, and pixel width.
2. Set those values as an export preset in your editor so every future image in that category exports correctly in one click.
3. Export a test image, check its actual file size, and adjust quality slightly until it lands at or under the target.
4. If an image still overshoots, resize the pixels first — shrinking dimensions beats crushing quality every time.
5. Re-check this sheet whenever a platform changes its display size, and update your presets to match.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do file size targets matter if my internet is fast?

Not every visitor shares your connection. Large images slow pages on mobile networks, hurt search rankings, and cost mobile users real data. Hitting a size target keeps your site fast for everyone, not just people on fast broadband.

### Should I use JPEG, PNG, or WebP?

Use JPEG or WebP for photos, PNG only when you need transparency or crisp text and lines, and WebP when your audience's browsers support it. This sheet recommends a format per row so you do not have to decide from scratch each time.

### What if my image is slightly over the target?

A small overshoot is fine — targets are guides, not hard limits. If you are far over, resize the pixel dimensions first, then lower quality in small steps until the size comes down without visible damage.
