## What's inside this pack

The 50 formulas cover every headline archetype that earns clicks honestly. Listicle formulas ("[Number] Ways to...") package content as skimmable value. How-to formulas promise transformation with a clear process. Question formulas mirror the reader's own curiosity. Comparison and review formulas serve buyers. Story formulas ("How I...") leverage personal proof. And contrarian formulas ("Why [belief] is wrong") earn attention by challenging assumptions. Each formula names the reader, the promise, or both — because vague headlines like "Thoughts on Productivity" compete with no one and win against no one.

## How to use this pack

1. Pick the formula that matches your article's shape: listicle for roundups, how-to for tutorials, question for curiosity pieces.
2. Fill in the blanks with specifics — a real number, a named audience, a concrete benefit. Specificity is what earns the click.
3. Write 5 to 10 headline options using different formulas; your first instinct is rarely your strongest.
4. Check the length: keep the keyword and the promise inside the first 60 characters so nothing important truncates.
5. Sanity-check against the article: the headline must promise exactly what the post delivers, or readers bounce.

Used as a habit, this pack removes the blank-page dread of title writing. Most professional bloggers keep a swipe file of headline patterns exactly like this — now you have one ready to go, and the strongest headline for every post is a few minutes away.

## Frequently asked questions

**What makes a headline get more clicks?**
Specificity. Headlines with a number, a named audience, and a concrete benefit consistently outperform vague ones. "17 Proven Ways to Save Money on Groceries" will beat "Great Ways to Save Money" every time.

**How long should a blog headline be?**
Under 60 characters is the safe zone — longer headlines get truncated in Google search results. If your headline runs long, put the keyword and the promise in the first 60 characters.

**Should the headline match the article exactly?**
Yes. The headline is a promise, and the article must keep it. Clickbait that overpromises earns a quick bounce, which hurts rankings and trust far more than the extra clicks are worth.
