Colorado keeps income tax refreshingly simple: one flat rate, no brackets, no marginal-rate math. For 2026, that rate is 4.4% applied to your Colorado taxable income. If you have ever wrestled with a progressive state's bracket tables, the Centennial State will feel like a vacation. This guide explains exactly how the flat rate works, the standard deduction that shrinks your taxable income first, a worked example, and a free calculator for an instant estimate.

## How the flat 4.4% rate works

"Flat" means every dollar of taxable income is taxed at the same rate: **4.4%**. There are no brackets to climb and no thresholds where your rate changes. A dollar earned at $30,000 of income is taxed exactly the same as a dollar earned at $300,000.

The entire calculation is one line:

**Colorado tax = Colorado taxable income × 4.4%**

That simplicity is the point. You do not need to track which slice of income falls in which bracket, because there are no slices. The trade-off, if you want to call it that, is that lower earners pay the same rate as higher earners — there is no 0% or 2% bracket sheltering the first dollars of income the way some progressive states offer.

Colorado taxable income starts from your federal taxable income, adjusted for Colorado-specific additions and subtractions (things like certain retirement income subtractions the state allows). Then you subtract the standard deduction or your itemized deductions, and whatever remains gets the 4.4%.

## The standard deduction: $16,100 single / $32,200 joint

Before the 4.4% applies, you shrink your taxable income. For 2026, Colorado's standard deduction mirrors the federal amounts:

- **Single filers:** $16,100
- **Married filing jointly:** $32,200

You can itemize instead if your itemized deductions — mortgage interest, charitable contributions, state and local taxes up to the federal cap, and similar — exceed the standard amount. Most filers take the standard deduction because it is larger than their itemizable expenses.

Because the deduction comes off before the flat rate applies, it is worth real money: a single filer taking the $16,100 standard deduction saves $16,100 × 4.4% = **$708.40** in Colorado tax.

## Worked example: $60,000 single filer

A single filer with $60,000 of income taking the standard deduction:

**Step 1: Subtract the standard deduction.** $60,000 − $16,100 = **$43,900** of Colorado taxable income.

**Step 2: Apply the flat rate.** $43,900 × 4.4% = **$1,931.60**.

**Step 3: Subtract credits.** Colorado offers various credits (for example, tied to dependents, property taxes for qualifying filers, and specific state programs). Subtract any credits you qualify for from the $1,931.60 to get your final tax.

That is the whole calculation — three steps, one multiplication. Compare that with a progressive state where you would be slicing income across five or six brackets.

## Flat tax vs. bracket states: what changes for you

If you moved to Colorado from a bracket state, three things feel different:

1. **No bracket planning.** Strategies like shifting income between years to stay under a bracket threshold do not apply — the rate is the same regardless.
2. **Deductions are worth a flat 4.4%.** Every $1,000 of deductions saves exactly $44 in tax, whether you earn $40,000 or $400,000. In progressive states, deductions are worth more to higher earners.
3. **Withholding is straightforward.** Your employer's Colorado withholding is a simple percentage of taxable wages, which makes paycheck math easy to verify.

One more Colorado quirk worth knowing: the state constitution's TABOR (Taxpayer's Bill of Rights) sometimes triggers refunds or temporary rate adjustments when state revenue exceeds its cap. The 4.4% rate is the baseline for 2026; TABOR-related refunds, when they happen, typically arrive as separate refund mechanisms rather than changing the rate itself.

## Filing basics: what to have ready

Colorado individual returns are generally due April 15. Gather your W-2s and 1099s, records of estimated tax payments, and documentation for any credits you plan to claim — Colorado's credit list is longer than most flat-tax states', covering everything from dependent-related credits to conservation and childcare incentives. If you earned income in multiple states, you will need each state's return to sort out credits for taxes paid elsewhere. E-filing through the Department of Revenue's free system or commercial software gets most refunds out within a few weeks.
