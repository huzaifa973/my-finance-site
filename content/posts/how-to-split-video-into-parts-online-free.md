A two-hour webinar, a long gameplay recording, a full event video — long videos are hard to watch and harder to share. Splitting them into parts turns one unwieldy file into digestible clips: perfect for social media, course modules, highlight reels, or just finding the good bits later.

You don't need Premiere or a subscription. Our free [video trimmer](/tools/video-splitter/) cuts segments from any video right in your browser — the file never leaves your device, and there's no signup or watermark.

## Why split videos into parts

**Social media demands it.** TikTok, Reels, and Shorts cap video length, and algorithms reward completion rate — a 45-second clip of the best moment outperforms a 20-minute upload every time. One long recording can become a week of posts.

**Courses and tutorials need it.** Nobody wants "Module 7" buried at 1:42:00 of a single file. Splitting a recording into titled parts makes content navigable, lets learners resume easily, and looks far more professional.

**Sharing gets easier.** A 2 GB file won't send over most messaging apps or email. Five 400 MB parts (or better, short highlight clips) go through fine — and recipients actually watch them.

**Editing gets safer.** Working with small clips instead of one giant timeline means faster previews, quicker exports, and less risk of losing work to a crash.

## How to split a video into parts (step by step)

**1. Upload your video.** Open the free [video trimmer](/tools/video-splitter/) and drop in your file. MP4, WebM, and most common formats work, and processing happens locally — even large files stay private and don't eat upload time.

**2. Find your cut points.** Scrub through the timeline and note where each part should start and end. Good split points: topic changes, natural pauses, scene changes, or fixed intervals (every 10 minutes for a lecture, every 60–90 seconds for social clips).

**3. Cut the first segment.** Set the start and end markers around your first part, then export it. Name the file clearly as you go — `webinar-part-01-intro.mp4` beats `clip-final-v2.mp4` when you're juggling ten exports.

**4. Repeat for each part.** Move the markers to the next segment and export again. Overlap your cut points by a second or two if the parts will be watched in sequence — a hard cut mid-sentence feels broken, while a tiny overlap feels intentional.

**5. Organize the outputs.** Keep a consistent naming scheme with zero-padded numbers (part-01, part-02 … part-10) so files sort correctly. Store the original untouched as your master.

## Keyframe cuts vs re-encoding: what to choose

This is the technical detail that determines speed and quality:

- **Keyframe (lossless) splitting** cuts only at keyframes — the full reference frames in the video stream. It's nearly instant and preserves the original quality bit-for-bit. The downside: cuts can only land where keyframes exist (typically every 2–10 seconds), so timing is approximate.
- **Re-encoded splitting** decodes and re-encodes the video, allowing frame-accurate cuts at any millisecond. It takes longer (minutes for long videos) and loses a tiny amount of quality — one generation of re-encoding at a good bitrate is visually negligible.

Rule of thumb: use keyframe cuts when you're chopping a long recording into rough parts and speed matters. Use re-encoded cuts when precise timing matters — dialogue, music, or clips where a mid-word cut would be noticeable.

## Splitting strategies for common goals

**For TikTok/Reels/Shorts:** Pull 30–90 second self-contained moments. Each clip needs its own hook in the first 2 seconds — don't assume viewers saw part 1. Add captions; most viewers watch muted.

**For online courses:** Split at topic boundaries and title each part descriptively ("3.2 — Setting up the database"). Keep parts under 15 minutes; completion rates fall off a cliff beyond that.

**For highlights:** Watch at 1.5–2x speed, mark timestamps of the best moments as you go, then cut just those. A 10-minute highlight reel beats a 2-hour raw upload for every audience.

**For archiving:** Split very long recordings (security footage, full-day events) into 30–60 minute chunks. Smaller files are easier to back up, move, and repair if one gets corrupted.
