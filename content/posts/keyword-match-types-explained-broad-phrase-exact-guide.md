If you have ever pasted a keyword list into Google Ads and watched your budget evaporate on irrelevant clicks, the culprit was almost certainly the match type — not the keywords. Broad, phrase, and exact match are the three levers that decide how closely a search query must relate to your keyword before your ad shows. Pick wrong and you pay for traffic you never wanted; pick right and every click arrives pre-qualified.

This guide explains what each match type actually does in 2026, with real examples, when to use each one, and how to convert a keyword list between formats in seconds.

## What keyword match types actually control

Think of a match type as a net with a different mesh size. Your keyword is the net; the search query is the fish. **Broad match** is a wide net — it catches anything related to your keyword's meaning, including synonyms and related searches. **Phrase match** is a medium net — the query must include your keyword's meaning in the same order. **Exact match** is a tight net — the query must carry the same meaning or intent as your keyword.

Google's matching is semantic now, not literal. In the old days exact match meant the query had to be character-identical. Today Google allows close variants — plurals, misspellings, word reorderings, even implied intent. The mesh sizes still matter enormously, but they describe meaning-distance, not string-distance.

## Broad match: maximum reach, minimum control

Broad match is the default and the loosest option. Write your keyword as plain text — `running shoes` — and Google may show your ad for queries like "best sneakers for marathon training," "Nike running footwear sale," or even "comfortable shoes for standing all day." The system interprets meaning, so synonyms, related concepts, and implied needs all count.

**When broad match shines:** discovery. Broad match is a research tool disguised as a targeting option. It surfaces search terms you would never have brainstormed, which you can then harvest into exact-match lists. It also works well with Smart Bidding, which uses your conversion data to filter the noise automatically.

**The danger:** budget bleed. Broad match will happily spend your money on tangentially related queries. The fix is not to avoid it but to tame it — run broad match in its own campaign, cap the budget, review the search terms report weekly, and add negatives aggressively.

## Phrase match: the balanced middle

Phrase match sits between the two. Write your keyword in quotes — `"running shoes"` — and your ad shows for queries that include the keyword's meaning in the same word order, with extra words before or after: "buy running shoes online," "best running shoes for flat feet," "running shoes near me." What it excludes are queries that split or reorder the meaning, like "shoes for running on the beach" (arguably) or "running clothing and shoes."

**When phrase match shines:** scaling winners. Once exact match tells you which keywords convert, phrase match captures the natural variations around them without opening the floodgates. It is the workhorse match type for most small accounts — precise enough to keep intent tight, loose enough to catch real-world phrasing.

## Exact match: maximum control, minimum reach

Exact match is the precision instrument. Write your keyword in brackets — `[running shoes]` — and your ad shows for queries with the same meaning or intent: "running shoes," "running shoe," "shoes for running." Google ignores plurals, misspellings, and word order, but it rejects anything that changes the intent — "running shoes vs walking shoes" would not match, because the intent is comparison, not purchase.

**When exact match shines:** budgets with zero tolerance for waste, and keywords where intent is money. Brand terms, high-intent buyer phrases ("buy," "price," "near me"), and your proven converters belong in exact match.

**The limitation:** low volume. Exact match misses the long-tail variations that phrase and broad would catch. Running exact-only means you see a clean but narrow slice of demand.

## Putting them together: a simple strategy

Most successful small accounts run a tiered structure, and it is simpler than it sounds:

1. **Start with exact and phrase.** Build your initial campaigns around 10–30 core keywords in exact and phrase match. Intent is clear, budgets stay efficient, and the data you collect is clean.
2. **Mine the search terms report.** Every week, pull the actual queries that triggered your ads. Queries that convert get promoted to exact match; queries that waste money become negatives.
3. **Add broad match for discovery.** Once you have conversion data feeding Smart Bidding, launch a small broad-match campaign with its own capped budget. Let it find the queries you missed, then harvest the winners back into exact.
4. **Layer negative keywords at every level.** Negatives are the real control panel. A shared negative list — job seekers ("jobs," "careers"), freebie hunters ("free" unless you sell free), DIYers ("how to make") — protects every match type at once.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between broad, phrase, and exact match keywords?
Broad match matches queries related to your keyword's meaning, including synonyms. Phrase match matches queries that include your keyword's meaning in the same word order. Exact match matches queries with the same meaning or intent as your keyword. Broad gives reach, exact gives precision.

### Which keyword match type should beginners use?
Start with exact and phrase match. They keep budgets tight and intent clear while you learn what your audience searches for. Add broad match later, paired with good negative keywords, once you have conversion data.

### Is broad match still worth using in 2026?
Yes — Google's semantic matching has made broad match far smarter than the old anything-goes version. With tight budgets it needs careful negative keywords and conversion tracking, but it can surface queries you would never have thought to target.
