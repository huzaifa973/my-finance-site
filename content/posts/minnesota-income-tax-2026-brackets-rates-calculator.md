Minnesota has one of the most progressive income tax systems in the Midwest, with four brackets running from 5.35% to 9.85% — and the top rate is among the highest in the nation. For 2026, the rates themselves did not change, but the bracket thresholds moved upward with inflation, and the standard deduction rose too. That combination means a little more breathing room in your take-home pay, even if your salary did not change.

This guide walks you through the official 2026 brackets for every filing status, the standard deduction and dependent exemption, how the calculation works step by step, and how to get an exact estimate in seconds with our free calculator.

## How Minnesota's progressive income tax works

"Progressive" means your tax rate climbs as your income climbs — but only on the slice of income inside each bracket. This is the idea of *marginal* rates, and it is the single most misunderstood part of state taxes. If you are a single filer earning $120,000 of taxable income, you do not pay 7.85% on all of it. You pay 5.35% on the first $33,310, 6.80% on the next slice up to $109,430, and 7.85% only on the final $10,570. Your effective rate — what you actually pay as a share of income — is much lower than your top marginal rate.

Minnesota starts from your federal adjusted gross income (AGI), then applies its own standard deduction or itemized deductions, plus dependent exemptions, to reach Minnesota taxable income. With the exception of the state and local tax deduction, Minnesota generally allows the same itemized deductions as the federal return.

Note how wide the brackets are compared with many states: a single filer does not reach the top 9.85% rate until taxable income passes $203,150. That is why Minnesota's system feels progressive in practice, not just on paper — middle-income filers spend most of their income in the two lowest brackets.

## Standard deduction and dependent exemption for 2026

Before any bracket rate applies, you shrink your taxable income with deductions:

- **Single:** $15,300 standard deduction
- **Married filing jointly:** $30,600
- **Married filing separately:** $15,300
- **Head of household:** $23,000
- **Dependent exemption:** $5,300 per dependent

Minnesota sets its own standard deduction, which is separate from the federal amount — for 2026, Minnesota's single standard deduction of $15,300 sits close to the federal figure but is not identical to it. Additional standard deduction amounts apply if you are 65 or older or blind; check the Department of Revenue's website for those figures.

Minnesota does not offer a separate personal exemption beyond the dependent exemption, so the standard deduction and dependent exemptions do most of the work of lowering taxable income for typical filers.

## How to calculate your Minnesota income tax by hand

Follow these steps with a worked example: a single filer with $100,000 of federal AGI and no dependents.

**Step 1: Start with federal AGI.** Our example starts at $100,000.

**Step 2: Subtract the standard deduction.** $100,000 − $15,300 = $84,700 of Minnesota taxable income.

**Step 3: Apply the brackets, slice by slice.**

- 5.35% on the first $33,310 = $1,782.09
- 6.80% on the next $51,390 ($84,700 − $33,310) = $3,494.52
- Total Minnesota tax = $5,276.61, roughly $5,277

**Step 4: Read the result.** The effective rate is about 5.28% of gross income — far below the 6.80% marginal rate this filer actually tops out at. That gap between marginal and effective rates is exactly why "I moved into a higher bracket" rarely means what people fear it means.

## What else affects your Minnesota bill

**Social Security benefits.** Minnesota fully subtracts taxable Social Security benefits at federal AGI up to $86,410 for single filers ($110,780 joint) for 2026, with the subtraction phasing out above those thresholds. This is a meaningful break for retirees that many other states do not offer in the same form.

**Capital gains.** Minnesota taxes capital gains as ordinary income — there is no preferential state rate, so gains can be taxed up to 9.85%. An additional 1% tax applies to net investment income above $1 million.

**Itemized deductions.** If your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction, itemizing saves money. Remember that itemized deductions phase out at higher incomes — for most filers the phase-out begins at $238,950 of federal AGI ($119,475 for married filing separately).

**Estimated payments.** If you will owe tax and you make estimated quarterly payments, use the 2026 brackets for payments due starting April 2026. Underpaying estimates can trigger penalties, so revisit your withholding whenever your income changes.

## Planning moves for 2026

A few moves are worth considering before year-end. Pre-tax 401(k) contributions, HSA contributions, and payroll health premiums lower your federal AGI, which lowers Minnesota tax too — for a filer in the 6.80% bracket, each traditional 401(k) dollar saves 6.8 cents in state tax on top of the federal savings. If you are near a bracket boundary, timing a bonus, a Roth conversion, or a capital-gain sale across the year-end line can keep income in a lower bracket. And if you itemize, bunching deductible expenses into a single year can push you over the standard deduction threshold.

None of this requires a professional for most filers — but if your situation involves business income, rental properties, or multi-state filings, a Minnesota tax professional earns their fee quickly.

These brackets apply to income earned in calendar year 2026, which most people report when filing 2026 returns in 2027. Keep your pay stubs, W-2s, and 1099s organized now, and filing season becomes a formality rather than a scramble.
