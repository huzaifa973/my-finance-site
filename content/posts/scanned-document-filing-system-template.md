A scanner without a system just creates a digital junk drawer: hundreds of files named scan001, scan002, and scan003, none of which you can find when you actually need them. This template gives you a complete filing system for scanned documents — folder structure, naming rules, scanning settings, and upkeep habits — so every document you scan lands in the right place and can be found in seconds. It works for households managing bills and medical records, freelancers handling receipts and contracts, and small offices going paperless. Adapt the folder names to your life, but keep the rules consistent. The real payoff comes months later, when you need a document urgently and find it in seconds instead of digging through drawers or scrolling through a camera roll. A filing system earns its keep on the worst day, not the day you build it — so build it once, build it properly, and let it quietly save you time for years.

## What's inside this resource

The template has four parts. First, the folder structure: twelve main folders that cover nearly every document a household or small business scans. Second, subfolder examples that show how to break big folders down without overcomplicating them. Third, the naming rules — the single most important part, because a good filename makes documents searchable even without special software. Fourth, scanning settings and maintenance habits that keep the system tidy year after year. Copy the whole template into your own notes, rename what you need, and start using it with your very next scan.

## How to use this resource

1. Create the twelve main folders first, then add subfolders only for folders that already hold many files.
2. Rename your existing scans using the naming rules, starting with the documents you search for most.
3. Scan one small pile per session — a drawer, a folder, a month of mail — rather than attempting everything at once.
4. Set a weekly five-minute habit: file anything scanned that week and clear any 'to sort' pile.
5. Run the yearly review every January: archive old years, shred what you no longer need, and confirm your backup works.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I scan in color or black and white?

Color at 300 DPI for documents you may print or prove later, like contracts and certificates. Grayscale at 200 DPI is fine for everyday reference and saves storage space.

### Is a scanned copy legally as good as the original?

It depends on the document and your country. For most everyday purposes a clear scan is accepted, but keep paper originals of wills, deeds, and identity documents.

### How do I handle documents that belong in two folders?

File it in the most important folder and add a shortcut or note in the other. One real copy plus pointers beats two copies that drift out of sync.
