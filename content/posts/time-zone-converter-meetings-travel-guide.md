A 3 PM meeting in New York is 8 PM in London, midnight in Dubai, and 4 AM in Tokyo. Get one conversion wrong and someone misses the call — or worse, joins at 4 AM furious. Time zones are simple arithmetic wrapped in genuinely confusing edge cases: daylight saving, half-hour offsets, and the International Date Line. This guide gives you a reliable system for meetings and travel so the math is right every time.

## The 3 rules of time zone conversion

**Rule 1: Always convert with the date, not just the time.** Offsets change with daylight saving. "3 PM New York" means UTC−5 in winter and UTC−4 in summer — convert "3 PM on March 10" instead, and the DST question answers itself.

**Rule 2: Think in UTC, speak in local.** Convert each participant's time to UTC first, then to their local zone. UTC never observes daylight saving, so it is the only fixed reference point. (You will also see it called GMT — for conversion purposes they are interchangeable.)

**Rule 3: Name the zone, not just the offset.** "UTC+5" is ambiguous — it could be Pakistan (no DST) or a zone that shifts seasonally. "Asia/Karachi" or "America/New_York" is exact. When scheduling, write the city: "3 PM New York time" beats "3 PM EST" (which is wrong half the year anyway).

## Converting for meetings: the workflow

1. **Pick a candidate time** in your own zone.
2. **Run it through a time zone converter** with each participant's city and the meeting date. Free converters and world-clock tools handle the DST math for you — never do offsets from memory for important meetings.
3. **Sanity-check the results.** Is anyone outside 7 AM–9 PM local? Flag it before sending.
4. **Send calendar invites with time zones attached**, not plain text times. A proper invite auto-adjusts if a participant travels; "let's meet at 3 PM" in an email does not.
5. **Confirm in two zones** in the invite description: "3:00 PM New York / 8:00 PM London" — the redundancy catches errors.

For recurring meetings, **rotate the inconvenient slot**. If the Asia–US overlap always lands at someone's 9 PM, alternate which side takes the hit each quarter. Shared inconvenience is a team-health issue, not just politeness.

## Finding the least-bad global meeting slot

True overlap across the US, Europe, and Asia barely exists. The standard compromise windows:

- **US East + Europe:** 8–11 AM Eastern / 2–5 PM Central Europe. Comfortable for everyone.
- **US West + Asia:** 4–6 PM Pacific / 8–10 AM Singapore–Tokyo next day. Early for Asia, late-ish for the US.
- **All three regions:** 7–9 AM Eastern / 1–3 PM Europe / 8–10 PM Asia. Nobody loves it; everybody survives it.

When even the compromise fails, **go asynchronous**: record a video update, keep a shared doc, and reserve live time for decisions only. The best global meeting is often no meeting.

## Time zones for travel

- **Book everything in destination local time.** Flights, hotels, tours, and restaurant reservations all run on local time. Your confirmation emails do too — double-check which zone each one uses.
- **Set your phone to show two clocks:** destination time and home time. One glance answers both "when is dinner?" and "can I call home now?"
- **Crossing the date line:** flying west across the Pacific you can land "before" you took off; flying east you lose a day. Check the arrival *date*, not just the time, when booking onward connections.
- **The 24-hour rule for important events.** Arrive a full day before weddings, conferences, or anything unmissable — a missed connection plus a time-zone misread is how people miss their own events.
- **Jet lag strategy:** shift sleep toward destination time a few days before flying east; get morning sunlight on arrival. The rule of thumb is one day of adjustment per time zone crossed.

## The classic mistakes (and how to dodge them)

- **"EST" in summer.** Eastern Time is EDT (UTC−4) from March to November. Writing EST year-round guarantees a one-hour error half the year — write the city instead.
- **Forgetting the Southern Hemisphere flips DST.** When New York springs forward, Sydney falls back. US–Australia offsets swing by *two* hours across the year.
- **Half-hour and 45-minute zones.** India (UTC+5:30), Nepal (UTC+5:45), and parts of Australia do not sit on whole hours — mental math fails here; use a converter.
- **Assuming the offset never changes.** Arizona ignores DST, most of the US observes it. Same country, different rules.
- **Scheduling during the transition weekend.** Twice a year there is an hour that happens twice (or never). ## Frequently asked questions

**How do I convert time zones for a meeting?**
Use a time zone converter: enter your local time and date, add each participant's city, and read off their local times. Always specify the date too — daylight saving can shift offsets by an hour.

**What is the best time for a meeting across US, Europe, and Asia?**
There is no perfect slot — aim for the least-bad one: typically 7–9 AM US Eastern / 1–3 PM Central Europe / 8–10 PM Singapore or Tokyo. Rotate the pain across recurring meetings.

**Why did my meeting time shift by an hour?**
Daylight saving transitions. The US, Europe, and Australia change clocks on different dates, creating 2–3 week windows each year where the usual offset is wrong by an hour. Calendar invites with time zones attached handle this automatically.

**Should I schedule travel in local time or home time?**
Local time at the destination, always — flights, hotels, and bookings all run on local time. Keep one world clock showing home time on your phone for calls back.
