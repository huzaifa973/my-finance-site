One video, five platforms — and every platform wants a different shape. YouTube wants wide 16:9, TikTok and Reels want tall 9:16, Instagram feed likes square. Cropping is how you reframe one recording for all of them, and you can do it free in your browser.

## What video cropping actually does

Cropping cuts pixels off the edges of your video — it does not stretch or squeeze anything. A 1920×1080 (16:9) video cropped to 1080×1080 (1:1) keeps the middle 1080 pixels and discards 420 pixels from each side. Subjects keep their natural proportions; you just see less of the scene.

This is different from **resizing** (changing the pixel dimensions of the whole frame) and **letterboxing** (adding black bars). Cropping is the right tool when you want to reframe or change aspect ratio.

## Cropping vs. reframing tricks

Static crops are not your only option. A slow **zoom-in during the crop** (the Ken Burns effect) adds motion to still-feeling footage, and **keyframed crops** let the crop box follow a moving subject — effectively a pan. Both are just crops that change over time, and most browser croppers support at least a fixed zoom. Use them sparingly: one reframing move per clip is plenty.

## Aspect ratio cheat sheet

- **16:9 (1920×1080):** YouTube, Vimeo, presentations, TV. The default "landscape" shape.
- **9:16 (1080×1920):** TikTok, Reels, Shorts, Stories. Full-screen vertical.
- **1:1 (1080×1080):** Instagram/Facebook feed posts. Maximum feed real estate for a square.
- **4:5 (1080×1350):** Instagram portrait posts — actually takes up *more* screen space than square in the feed.
- **21:9:** cinematic ultrawide for film-style content (adds letterbox bars on most screens).

## Cropping strategy: one shoot, every platform

Smart creators shoot once and crop many times:

1. **Shoot wider than you need.** Record in 16:9 at the highest resolution available — 4K if possible. Extra pixels are cropping headroom.
2. **Keep subjects centered.** Frame interviews and talking-head shots with the subject in the middle third, so both vertical and square crops keep them.
3. **Crop vertical first.** The 9:16 crop is the most destructive (you lose ~75% of a 16:9 frame), so nail the vertical framing, then derive square and wide versions.
4. **Check text and captions.** Burned-in text near the edges of a wide video gets amputated in vertical crops. Keep on-screen text in the central "safe zone."

## Quality tips

**Start with more pixels than you need.** Cropping a 4K video to 1080×1920 vertical still leaves you full HD. Cropping a 1080p video to vertical leaves you 608×1080 — visibly soft on phones.

**Do not upscale the crop.** Exporting a 608-pixel-wide crop as 1080 wide does not add detail — it just smears the pixels you have. Export at the crop's native resolution.

**Watch the bitrate.** Cropped videos re-encode on export. Use a healthy bitrate (8–12 Mbps for 1080p) so the re-encode does not introduce blockiness.

**Crop before captions.** Add subtitles and graphics *after* cropping, positioned for the final aspect ratio — not before, where they may get cut.
