You don't need a paid app to budget well. Some of the best budgeting tools in the UK are free spreadsheets — and unlike apps, they never charge a subscription, never sell your data, and never lock your history behind a paywall.

Here are the 9 best free options for UK beginners in 2026, what each one is good at, and how to pick.

## 1. MoneyHelper Budget Planner

The free budget planner from MoneyHelper (the UK's official money guidance service) is the best starting point for most beginners. It's web-based, built specifically for UK finances, and walks you through income, spending, debts, and savings with prompts for categories beginners forget — council tax, TV licence, water bills.

**Best for:** Your very first budget. **Cost:** Free, no account needed.

## 2. Google Sheets Monthly Budget Template

Google's built-in monthly budget template (File → New → From template gallery in Google Sheets) gives you a clean income-vs-expenses layout with a summary dashboard. Fully customisable, works on any device, and auto-saves.

**Best for:** A simple, flexible monthly budget you control. **Cost:** Free with a Google account.

## 3. Microsoft Excel Personal Budget Template

Excel's built-in personal budget template is more polished than Google's, with nicer charts and a proper annual summary view. Free through Excel for the web with a Microsoft account — no Microsoft 365 subscription needed.

**Best for:** People who already live in Excel. **Cost:** Free via Excel for the web.

## 4. Zero-Based Budget Spreadsheet

A zero-based template assigns every pound a job until income minus spending equals zero. Google "free zero-based budget spreadsheet UK" and you'll find several solid community versions for Google Sheets. This is the format to graduate to once the basic monthly budget feels easy.

**Best for:** Tight budgets where every pound matters. **Cost:** Free.

## 5. Debt Payoff Spreadsheet (Snowball / Avalanche)

A dedicated debt tracker lists each debt's balance, interest rate, and minimum payment, then shows your payoff date under the snowball (smallest first) or avalanche (highest interest first) method. Watching the "debt-free date" move closer each month is powerful motivation.

**Best for:** Anyone with credit cards, loans, or overdrafts. **Cost:** Free.

## 6. 50/30/20 Budget Calculator Sheet

A simple sheet that takes your take-home pay and auto-calculates your 50/30/20 targets — needs, wants, savings — then lets you log actual spending against each bucket. Our own [budget calculator](/tools/budget-calculator/) does the same math instantly if you don't want to build the sheet yourself.

**Best for:** Visual learners who like the 50/30/20 framework. **Cost:** Free.

## 7. Annual Bills & Sinking Funds Tracker

The budget-killer nobody plans for: car insurance, MOT, Christmas, annual subscriptions. This sheet lists every irregular yearly cost, divides by 12, and tracks a monthly "sinking fund" contribution so December doesn't wreck January.

**Best for:** Stopping surprise bills from breaking the budget. **Cost:** Free.

## 8. Household Budget Spreadsheet (Couples / Families)

A shared Google Sheet both partners can edit, with columns for who pays what and a joint-vs-personal split. Transparency kills most money arguments — you stop debating and start looking at the same numbers.

**Best for:** Couples and shared households. **Cost:** Free.

## 9. Simple Expense Tracker

Not a full budget — just a daily log of what you spent, with a pivot-friendly layout. Use it for 30 days when you have no idea where your money goes. The data becomes the foundation for a real budget in month two.

**Best for:** The "where does it all go?" phase. **Cost:** Free.

## How to choose the right one

| Your situation | Start with |
|---|---|
| Never budgeted before | MoneyHelper Budget Planner (#1) |
| Want full control | Google Sheets monthly template (#2) |
| Tight income, every pound counts | Zero-based sheet (#4) |
| Drowning in debt | Debt payoff tracker (#5) |
| Surprise bills keep wrecking you | Sinking funds tracker (#7) |
| Budgeting as a couple | Shared household sheet (#8) |
| No idea where money goes | Expense tracker (#9) for 30 days |

Don't download all nine. Pick the one that matches your situation, use it for two months, then add a second sheet only if you have a specific gap.

## Making a spreadsheet actually work: 5 rules

1. **Use take-home pay, not salary.** Budget from what lands in your account after tax and National Insurance.
2. **Update it weekly, not monthly.** Five minutes every Sunday beats a painful hour at month-end.
3. **Include a buffer line.** £50–£100 labelled "stuff happens" absorbs the costs you forgot.
4. **Keep categories under 15.** More categories = more quitting. Group small things under "personal".
5. **Review and adjust monthly.** A budget is a living document. The first version is always wrong; version three is usually right.

### FAQ

**Q:** What is the best free budgeting spreadsheet for UK beginners?

A: The MoneyHelper budget planner is the best starting point for UK beginners because it's built for UK finances (council tax, National Insurance, UK benefit categories) and walks you through income, spending, and savings step by step. Google Sheets monthly budget templates are the best flexible option once you know your categories.

**Q:** Are Google Sheets budgeting templates really free?

A: Yes. Google Sheets is free with any Google account, and the template gallery plus community-built budgeting templates cost nothing. Microsoft Excel's built-in budget templates are free if you already have Excel or use Excel for the web, which is free with a Microsoft account.

**Q:** How do I make a budget spreadsheet in the UK?

A: List monthly take-home pay at the top, then list all outgoings grouped as essentials (rent, council tax, utilities, food, transport), debts, and discretionary spending. Subtract spending from income — the remainder is your savings target. UK-specific lines to include: council tax, TV licence, National Insurance, and any benefit income.

**Q:** Spreadsheet vs budgeting app — which is better?

A: Spreadsheets win on cost (free), privacy (your data stays yours), and flexibility (custom categories). Apps win on automation (bank syncing, auto-categorisation). Beginners who want to understand their money should start with a spreadsheet for 2–3 months, then switch to an app if manual tracking gets tedious.
