A folder of photos from a birthday, a wedding, a trip — they deserve better than sitting in a camera roll. A slideshow video turns them into something people actually watch: photos that glide across the screen with transitions, set to music, wrapped in a video file you can share anywhere. You do not need editing software or any experience. A free browser-based slideshow maker does the heavy lifting, and the whole project takes about fifteen minutes. Here is how to plan it, build it, and export a video you will be proud to share.

## Plan before you build

<!-- ADSENSE: in-article ad slot -->

Ten minutes of planning saves an hour of fiddling:

- **Pick a story.** Chronological (the trip, day by day), thematic (all the food shots together), or emotional arc (quiet opening, energetic middle, warm ending). A slideshow with a shape beats a random shuffle.
- **Cull ruthlessly.** 30 great photos beat 80 mediocre ones. Remove duplicates, blurry shots, and near-identical bursts — keep one of each moment.
- **Choose the music first.** The track sets the pace. An upbeat song wants quick 3-second cuts; a gentle piano piece wants slow 5-second fades.
- **Mind the aspect ratio.** Landscape photos suit 16:9 video; a batch of portrait phone shots may look better in a square or vertical export.

## Build the slideshow step by step

Our [photo slideshow maker](/tools/photo-slideshow-maker/) runs entirely in your browser — your photos never leave your device:

1. **Upload your photos.** Drag in the culled set. They appear as a filmstrip you can reorder by dragging.
2. **Arrange the order.** Put the strongest photo first (it hooks viewers) and another strong one last (it is what they remember).
3. **Set the timing.** 3-5 seconds per photo is the sweet spot. Let key moments linger a beat longer; move briskly through sequences.
4. **Add transitions.** Simple crossfades or gentle slides between every photo. One transition style for the whole video looks professional; a different effect every three seconds looks chaotic.
5. **Add music.** Upload a track you have the rights to use — your own recording, royalty-free music, or a licensed track. Trim it to match the slideshow length and fade it out at the end.
6. **Add titles (sparingly).** An opening title and a closing credit are plenty. Text over every photo turns a slideshow into a presentation.
7. **Export as MP4.** H.264 encoding, 1080p if your photos are high-resolution. MP4 plays everywhere with the best quality-to-size balance.

### The 3-second rule of pacing
If a viewer can absorb the photo in 3 seconds, move on at 4. Lingering is only for photos with a story — the group shot where everyone is laughing, the landscape that took your breath away. Pace is what separates "nice photos" from "I watched the whole thing."

## Export settings that matter

Two settings decide how your slideshow looks everywhere else. **Resolution:** export at 1080p if your source photos are high-resolution — it future-proofs the video for TVs and YouTube. If the slideshow is WhatsApp-only, 720p keeps the file small enough to send as a media message. **Frame rate:** 30fps is the safe default; nothing in a photo slideshow needs more. Before sharing widely, watch the export once on your phone — the small screen reveals pacing problems, typos in titles, and music that is too loud or too quiet. Fix what you spot, re-export, and only then send it out.

## Mistakes that ruin slideshows

- **Too many photos.** Past 60 photos (about 4 minutes), attention collapses. Split long events into themed parts.
- **Mixed orientations without a plan.** Portrait photos in a landscape video get awkward blurred sidebars. Crop to a consistent frame or choose a vertical export for phone-shot collections.
- **Copyrighted music.** Uploading a slideshow with a chart hit to YouTube or Instagram can get it muted or blocked. Use royalty-free tracks for anything public.
- **The Ken Burns overdo.** Slow zoom-and-pan on every photo is a classic technique — applied to every single photo, it becomes seasickness. Use it on a few key images.
- **No ending.** Videos that just stop feel unfinished. A simple fade to black with a closing line ("Thanks for watching") gives the viewer permission to stop.
