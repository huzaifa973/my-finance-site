## What to put in a QR code

**Website links.** The classic use — send people from a poster, package, or card straight to your site, video, or social profile.

**WiFi passwords.** Print a WiFi QR code for your home or café and guests join in one scan instead of typing a 20-character password.

**Business cards.** Encode a vCard with your name, phone, email, and website. One scan saves the whole contact — no manual typing, no typos.

**Menus and catalogs.** Restaurants, salons, and shops use QR codes to link physical spaces to digital menus that update anytime.

**Payments and tips.** Link directly to your payment page or tip jar — street performers, freelancers, and small vendors all use this.

**Event details.** Encode the venue address, schedule link, or ticket page on invitations and posters.

## QR code best practices

**Size matters in print.** A QR code should be at least 2 x 2 cm (about 0.8 inches) to scan reliably — bigger for posters viewed at a distance. When in doubt, print larger.

**Always test-scan before publishing.** Scan with both an iPhone and an Android if you can. A code that doesn't scan is worse than no code.

**Keep the destination stable.** A static QR code encodes the link directly — if the link breaks, the code breaks. Use URLs you control and don't plan to delete.

**Add a call to action.** "Scan to see the menu" or "Scan for 10% off" dramatically increases scan rates versus a bare code. People scan when they know what they get.

**Mind the contrast.** Black on white scans best. Decorative low-contrast color schemes look nice but fail in poor lighting — and most scans happen in poor lighting.

## Static vs. dynamic QR codes

**Static QR codes** encode your content directly into the pattern. They're free forever, never expire, and can't be tracked or edited after creation. For a link that won't change — your homepage, a WiFi password, a vCard — static is the right choice, and it's what our free generator makes.

**Dynamic QR codes** encode a short redirect URL hosted by a service, which forwards to your real destination. The destination can be changed later and scans can be tracked — but the redirect depends on the service staying alive and usually requires a paid plan. If the service shuts down or your subscription lapses, the code dies.

Rule of thumb: use static codes unless you specifically need editable destinations or scan analytics. Most personal and small-business uses never need dynamic codes — and "free dynamic QR" offers that expire after a trial are a classic upsell trap.

## Tracking QR code scans

Static QR codes can't track scans — that's the trade-off for being free forever. If you need analytics (how many people scanned your flyer?), use a URL shortener with click stats or a UTM-tagged link as the QR destination; your analytics then capture every scan as a visit. For most uses this is plenty — you get the scan count without paying for dynamic QR services. Only upgrade to dynamic codes if you truly need to change the destination after printing.
