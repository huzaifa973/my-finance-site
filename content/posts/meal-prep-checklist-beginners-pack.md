Meal prep sounds like something other people do — people with color-coded containers and Sunday routines. But at its core it's just cooking once to eat well all week. The payoff is real: you spend less on takeout, eat healthier without daily decisions, and reclaim your weeknights. Most beginners fail not because cooking is hard, but because they skip the planning and shopping steps and end up with random ingredients and no plan.

This 30-step checklist gives beginners a complete weekly system: plan your menu, shop with a real list, prep efficiently in one session, store everything safely, and reheat without the sad-leftover experience. It's designed for real kitchens and real schedules — about two hours of cooking covers five days of lunches and dinners. Each step includes a copy button so you can build your own weekly routine from it.

## What's inside this checklist

The checklist has 30 steps in five phases. Phase 1 is planning: choosing meals, checking what you already have, and building a menu that shares ingredients. Phase 2 is shopping: writing the list by store section and buying smart. Phase 3 is the prep session itself: the order of operations that gets everything cooked in about two hours. Phase 4 is storage: cooling, portioning, labeling, and food-safety rules. Phase 5 is the eating week: reheating, mixing and matching, and the midweek check that keeps the system alive. Every step is one clear action.

## How to use this checklist

1. On Saturday or Sunday morning, run Phase 1: pick recipes, check the pantry, and write the menu.
2. Shop once using the Phase 2 list — grouped by section, with sauces and fresh herbs on it.
3. Block two hours for the Phase 3 prep session: longest item first, batch the chopping, season boldly.
4. Portion, label, and store per Phase 4 — and freeze anything past day four immediately.
5. During the week, follow Phase 5: reheat properly, mix and match, and do the Wednesday check.

Start smaller than you think. Your first week should be three lunches, not a full seven-day operation — success with a small batch teaches the system, and you scale up naturally. The second trick is embracing components over finished meals: a tray of roasted vegetables, a pot of rice, and two proteins become a dozen different bowls with different sauces. By week three, the whole thing runs on autopilot.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does meal-prepped food stay good?**
Most cooked meals stay safe 3-4 days in the fridge at or below 40°F (4°C). Cooked grains and roasted vegetables hold well; fish is best within 2 days. When in doubt, freeze half the batch on prep day.

**What containers are best for meal prep?**
Glass containers with locking lids are the best all-around choice — they reheat evenly, don't stain, and last for years. Get one uniform size so they stack neatly, plus a few small ones for sauces and snacks.

**Do I have to eat the same meal every day?**
No — that's the fastest way to quit. Prep versatile components (a grain, two proteins, roasted vegetables, a sauce) and mix-and-match through the week. Same ingredients, different meals.
