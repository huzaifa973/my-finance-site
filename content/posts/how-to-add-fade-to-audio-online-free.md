## What fades do (and why they matter)

A **fade-in** ramps the volume up gradually from silence, so the listener's ear has a moment to adjust. A **fade-out** ramps it down to silence, so the ending feels intentional instead of accidental.

Professional audio uses fades everywhere: songs fade out instead of stopping dead, podcast segments fade under intro music, videos crossfade between scenes. Without fades, even great content sounds raw.

## When to use fades

**Podcast episodes.** Fade in the first second of your intro and fade out the outro so episodes don't start or end with a click.

**Music tracks and loops.** Fade the end of a track used under a video so it dissolves instead of cutting off mid-note.

**Voiceovers.** A gentle fade-in hides the "dead air" breath before someone starts speaking and removes room tone at the tail.

**Ringtones and alerts.** Fade-outs keep custom ringtones from jolting you with a hard stop.

## Fade duration cheat sheet

- **Speech / podcasts:** 1–2 seconds each end
- **Pop songs:** 3–5 second fade-out
- **Ambient / sleep audio:** 8–15 seconds for a dreamy dissolve
- **Sound effects:** 0.2–0.5 seconds — just enough to remove clicks

Remember: fades on both ends are the default, but sometimes you want only one. A fade-in with no fade-out (for a track that flows into another) or vice versa is totally valid — the tool lets you set each independently.

## Fade shapes: linear vs. exponential

Not all fades sound the same. The *shape* of the fade curve changes how the volume transition feels:

**Linear fades** change volume at a constant rate — a straight line from loud to silent. They're predictable and fine for most purposes, but can feel like they "drop off" suddenly near the end because human hearing is logarithmic, not linear.

**Exponential (logarithmic) fades** follow how ears actually perceive loudness, dropping quickly at first and easing into silence. They sound smoother and more natural for music fade-outs — this is the curve professional audio tools default to.

**S-curve fades** ease in at both ends of the fade, which works beautifully for crossfading two clips together — the outgoing audio melts away while the incoming audio blooms.

For simple start/end fades on speech, any shape works. For music, exponential fades sound noticeably more professional.

## Common fading mistakes

**Fading too long on speech.** A 5-second fade-in on a podcast intro buries the first words. Speech needs short fades — 1 to 2 seconds.

**Forgetting the fade-out.** Creators remember the intro and neglect the ending. An abrupt stop undoes all the polish of a good fade-in.

**Fading the whole track.** A fade should touch only the edges. If the middle of your audio is getting quieter, something went wrong — check that your fade durations are shorter than the file.

## Fades in video editing

The same fade principles apply to video soundtracks: fade in the audio under your opening shot and fade out under the end card. Most viewer drop-off happens in the first seconds — a harsh audio start is a surprisingly common reason people click away. When you join multiple clips, add tiny 0.2-second fades at each cut point to eliminate the clicks and pops that betray an edit. These micro-fades are invisible to the ear but remove the amateur giveaways.
