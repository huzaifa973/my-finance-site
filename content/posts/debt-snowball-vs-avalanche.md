If you have more than one debt, you've faced the big question: **which one do I attack first?** Two methods dominate every personal finance book, and they give different answers. One saves the most money. The other gets you your first win fastest.

This post settles it with real numbers on the same set of debts — so you can see exactly what each method costs in time and interest.

## The two methods, in 30 seconds

**Debt snowball:** list debts from smallest balance to largest. Pay minimums on everything, throw all extra cash at the smallest. When it dies, roll its payment into the next smallest. Momentum first.

**Debt avalanche:** list debts from highest interest rate to lowest. Pay minimums on everything, throw all extra cash at the highest APR. When it dies, roll its payment into the next highest rate. Math first.

Both use the same engine — minimums everywhere, extra payments laser-focused on one target, freed payments "rolling" into the next. The only difference is the order.

## The example debts we'll use

<!-- ADSENSE: in-article slot -->

Meet our example borrower. Three debts, $8,000 total, and $320/month available for debt payments:

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Card A | $700 | 19.99% | $25 |
| Card B | $2,800 | 29.99% | $70 |
| Card C | $4,500 | 24.99% | $115 |
| **Total** | **$8,000** | — | **$210** |

That leaves $110/month in extra payments to aim at one target. (These are simplified estimates — real payoffs vary with compounding timing and fees — but the comparison is apples-to-apples.)

## Round 1: The snowball (smallest balance first)

**Order: Card A → Card B → Card C**

- **Months 1–6:** All $110 extra goes to Card A ($135/month total). Card A dies around month 6. First win in half a year.
- **Months 7–23:** Card A's $135 rolls into Card B ($205/month total). Card B — the brutal 29.99% one — dies around month 23.
- **Months 24–37:** The full $320 attacks Card C. Debt-free around month 37.

**Snowball totals: ~37 months, roughly $3,850 in total interest.**

The psychology here is excellent: a dead debt in 6 months, then a second kill about a year and a half in. Each win frees a bigger payment for the next target — the "snowball" rolling downhill.

## Round 2: The avalanche (highest APR first)

**Order: Card B → Card C → Card A**

- **Months 1–20:** All $110 extra goes to Card B ($180/month total). The 29.99% balance gets crushed from day one and dies around month 20. No early win — just grinding.
- **Months 21–36:** $295/month attacks Card C. It falls around month 36.
- **Month 37:** The leftover $700 card at 19.99% — which has been quietly shrinking on minimums — takes one final payment.

**Avalanche totals: ~36 months, roughly $3,650 in total interest.**

## The verdict: avalanche wins on math, barely

| | Snowball | Avalanche |
|---|---|---|
| Payoff order | A → B → C | B → C → A |
| Time to debt-free | ~37 months | ~36 months |
| Total interest (approx.) | ~$3,850 | ~$3,650 |
| First debt killed | Month 6 | Month 20 |
| Difference | — | Saves ~$200 and ~1 month |

Here's the honest takeaway most articles won't give you: **the mathematical gap is usually small.** On typical consumer debts, the avalanche saves a few hundred dollars — real money, but not life-changing. Meanwhile, behavioral research consistently shows that people using the snowball are more likely to *finish* paying everything off, because early wins keep them engaged.

A method you abandon in month 8 costs infinitely more than a method that saves $200 but that you quit. That's the entire argument for the snowball in one sentence.

## The hybrid: quick win, then math

You don't have to marry one method. The smartest approach for many people:

1. **Start with the snowball** if you have a small balance you can kill in 1–3 months. Get the win. Feel the momentum.
2. **Switch to avalanche** once the quick wins are done and you're staring at the big, high-APR balances where interest savings actually matter.
3. **Re-rank quarterly.** Balances and rates change (looking at you, promotional APRs expiring). Re-sort your list every few months.

Also note: if one debt has a *dramatically* higher rate than the others — say a 29.99% store card vs a 9% personal loan — the avalanche's advantage grows, and targeting that rate first is clearly right even for snowball fans.

## Whichever you choose, do these three things

The method matters less than the setup around it:

- **Stop adding new debt.** Neither method works if balances keep growing. Cards come out of the wallet until you're done (more in our guide to [paying off credit card debt fast](/posts/pay-off-credit-card-debt-fast/)).
- **Automate the extra payment.** Schedule it for payday. Manual payments get "forgotten" exactly when money is tight.
- **Lower your rates first.** A 5-minute call to each issuer asking for a lower APR — or a [balance transfer](/posts/best-balance-transfer-cards-2026/) — can save more than the snowball/avalanche difference combined.

### FAQ

**Q:** Which is better: debt snowball or avalanche?

A: The avalanche is mathematically cheaper because it targets the highest interest rate first. The snowball is psychologically stronger because it delivers the fastest first win. For most people carrying high-interest debt, the best method is the one they'll actually stick with for the full payoff — which is usually the snowball.

**Q:** How much money does the avalanche save over the snowball?

A: Usually less than people expect — often a few hundred dollars on a typical multi-card payoff. The gap grows when the rate difference between your debts is large (for example, a 29% card vs a 12% loan) and shrinks when your rates are similar.

**Q:** Can I switch from snowball to avalanche halfway through?

A: Yes, absolutely. Your payoff order isn't a contract. Many people start with the snowball for quick wins, then switch to avalanche once momentum is established and the remaining balances are larger.

**Q:** Do snowball and avalanche work for all types of debt?

A: They work for any debt with a fixed balance and minimum payment: credit cards, personal loans, car loans, and student loans. They don't apply the same way to mortgages (where the strategy is usually different) or to debts already in collections (where negotiation matters more).
