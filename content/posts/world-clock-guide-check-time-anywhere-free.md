## Why time zones are so confusing

The Earth has 24 nominal time zones, but reality is messier: there are **38+ distinct UTC offsets** in use, including half-hour (India, UTC+5:30) and 45-minute (Nepal, UTC+5:45) oddities. Then daylight saving time shifts the offsets for much of the world — but not on the same dates (the US and EU spring forward weeks apart), and large countries like the US span six zones while China uses one.

The result: the offset between any two cities is not a fixed number you can memorize. New York to London is 5 hours in winter, 4 in summer — and during the weeks when one has sprung forward and the other hasn't, it's briefly something else. Doing this math by hand is how meetings get missed. A world clock does it from the authoritative time zone database, correctly, every time.

## Scheduling meetings across time zones (without the pain)

**Find the overlap first.** Use the world clock's side-by-side view to spot shared working hours. The classic workable windows: US East Coast ↔ Europe (mornings ET / afternoons Europe), US West Coast ↔ Asia (evenings PT / mornings Asia). US ↔ Asia same-day overlap is brutally thin — someone meets early or late.

**State times with zones, always.** "3pm" is meaningless globally. Write "3pm ET / 8pm GMT" or just use UTC. Calendar invites handle this automatically if everyone accepts them — the invite displays in each recipient's local zone — so prefer calendar invites over plain-text times.

**Rotate the pain.** If your team spans 12 hours, don't make the same people take the 6am call every week. Rotate meeting times so the inconvenience is shared — it's the kind thing and it keeps the remote folks engaged.

**Beware the DST transition weeks.** Twice a year, US–Europe offsets shift by an hour for a few weeks (they change clocks on different dates). Recurring meetings scheduled across the Atlantic will silently move an hour. Check the world clock the week of each transition.

**Confirm the day, not just the time.** A 9pm call in New York is 10am *the next day* in Tokyo. Date-line crossings flip the calendar — always confirm "Tuesday 9pm ET / Wednesday 10am JST" to avoid off-by-a-day disasters.

## World clock tricks for travelers

**Check before you call.** A 30-second world clock check before dialing internationally saves you from waking someone at 3am. Make it a reflex.

**Plan around arrival times.** When booking flights, convert arrival times to your *body's* time zone to judge how wrecked you'll be — landing at "2pm local" after a red-eye feels very different from landing at 2pm rested.

**Set a second clock.** Most phones let you display dual clocks (home + current location). When traveling, keep home time visible — it anchors medication schedules, bill payments, and calls home.

**Meeting planner mode.** For trips with packed schedules, lay out the whole visit in the destination's zone using a multi-city view. Jet-lagged brains can't do offset math reliably; let the tool do it.
