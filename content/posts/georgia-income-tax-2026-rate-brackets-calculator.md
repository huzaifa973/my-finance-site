Georgia used to have one of the more complicated state income tax systems — six graduated brackets topping out at 5.75%. Then it changed course: starting in 2024, Georgia switched to a **flat income tax**, with the rate scheduled to step down over several years. Here is how the flat tax works, what the 2026 rate means for your paycheck, and how to estimate what you owe.

## Georgia's flat tax: what changed

Before 2024, Georgia taxed income in graduated brackets — the more you earned, the higher the marginal rate, up to 5.75%. The reform replaced all of that with **one single rate applied to all taxable income**, no matter how much you make.

The legislature set a step-down schedule:

- **2024:** 5.39%
- **2025:** 5.19%
- **Through 2029:** scheduled to continue stepping down toward **4.99%**

Important caveat: the later cuts are **contingent on the state meeting revenue triggers** — if budget conditions are not met, a scheduled cut can be delayed. So while the direction is downward, the exact rate in any given year is not locked in stone until confirmed.

**What this means in practice:** tax math in Georgia is now simple multiplication. There are no brackets to look up, no marginal-rate layering. Your Georgia taxable income times the flat rate equals your state income tax.

## A simple flat-rate calculation example

Let us walk through an example so the math is concrete. Suppose the flat rate for the year is **5.19%** and your Georgia taxable income is **$60,000**:

$60,000 × 0.0519 = **$3,114**

That is the entire calculation. Compare that to the old bracket system, where you would have applied different rates to different slices of income and summed them up — the flat tax removes all of that.

A few more quick examples at 5.19%:

- $30,000 taxable income → **$1,557**
- $80,000 taxable income → **$4,152**
- $120,000 taxable income → **$6,228**

## Who benefits from the flat tax?

The flat tax treats all income levels the same, which shifts the math compared to the old graduated system:

- **Higher earners** generally benefit most: under the old system they paid up to 5.75% on top slices of income; now every dollar is taxed at the lower flat rate.
- **Middle earners** see modest savings as the rate steps down each year.
- **Lower earners** see smaller absolute savings, since they were already paying the lower bracket rates — but the annual step-down still trims their bill slightly each year.

One thing the flat tax does not change: **Georgia taxable income is not the same as your salary.** Deductions, exemptions, and retirement-income exclusions still reduce the income figure the rate applies to, so your effective tax on gross pay is lower than the flat rate suggests.

## What still affects your Georgia tax bill

The rate is flat, but the *base* it applies to has moving parts:

- **Standard deduction:** Georgia has its own standard deduction amounts, which reduce your taxable income before the rate applies.
- **Personal exemptions:** Georgia allows personal exemptions that further lower taxable income.
- **Retirement income exclusion:** Georgia is notably generous here — taxpayers 62 and older can exclude significant retirement income, and those 65+ even more. This is a major planning factor for retirees.
- **Federal conformity:** Georgia generally starts from your federal adjusted gross income, then applies state-specific adjustments.

Because the base matters as much as the rate, two people with the same salary can owe different amounts. A calculator that walks through the adjustments gives a much better estimate than rate-times-salary alone.

## How to verify the current rate

Tax law changes, and the step-down schedule depends on conditions being met. Before you file or make estimated payments:

1. **Check the Georgia Department of Revenue** (dor.georgia.gov) for the official current-year rate, forms, and instructions.
2. **Watch for legislation** — the General Assembly can pause or modify the step-down in any session.
3. **Check your withholding** — if your employer withholds Georgia tax from your paycheck, confirm the withholding tables reflect the current rate so you are not over- or under-withheld.
