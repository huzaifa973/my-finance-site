Massachusetts keeps things simpler than most states: one flat rate, no brackets to climb. But the "millionaire surtax" added in 2023 means high earners face a second layer — and short-term capital gains get their own higher rate. Here is exactly how the Massachusetts income tax works for 2026, who pays what, and how to estimate your bill in seconds.

## The flat 5% rate: how it works

Massachusetts taxes most income at a **flat 5%**. Wages, salaries, tips, business income, interest, dividends, rental income, and long-term capital gains all face the same 5% rate. There are no brackets — earning more never pushes you into a higher rate the way federal brackets do.

The math is straightforward:

1. Start with your Massachusetts gross income.
2. Subtract exemptions and deductions (more on those below) to get taxable income.
3. Multiply by 5%.

A single filer with $80,000 of wages and the $4,400 personal exemption owes 5% of $75,600 = **$3,780** (before any credits). Try the free tool: /tools/massachusetts-income-tax-calculator/ to run your own numbers instantly.

## The 4% millionaire surtax (9% combined above the threshold)

Since 2023, Massachusetts adds a **4% surtax on the portion of taxable income above $1,107,750** — the 2026 inflation-adjusted threshold (it was $1 million originally and rises with inflation each year). Income above that line faces 5% + 4% = **9% combined**.

Key details:

- **It applies to the excess only.** Earn $1,200,000 and the surtax hits just the $92,250 above the threshold — about $3,690 extra.
- **It is per taxpayer.** Married couples filing jointly do not get a doubled threshold the way some states allow; each spouse's income is measured against the threshold under the state's rules.
- **It covers all income types**, including the normally 5%-rated long-term gains and the 8.5%-rated short-term gains — the surtax stacks on top.
- **Estimated payments matter.** If a bonus, stock sale, or business windfall pushes you over the threshold, you may owe quarterly estimated tax to avoid underpayment penalties.

## Capital gains: 5% vs. 8.5%

Massachusetts splits capital gains by holding period:

- **Long-term gains** (assets held more than one year): taxed at the flat **5%**.
- **Short-term gains** (held one year or less): taxed at **8.5%**.

That 3.5-point gap is one of the biggest single-year holding incentives in any state's tax code — holding an asset just past the one-year mark can cut the state tax on the gain nearly in half. (The federal distinction matters too, but that is a separate calculation.)

Collectibles and certain other gains follow the same long/short split. Day traders and crypto flippers: virtually all of your gains land in the 8.5% bucket.

## Exemptions and deductions that lower your bill

Before the 5% rate applies, Massachusetts lets you subtract:

- **Personal exemption:** **$4,400** for single filers, $8,800 for married filing jointly, $6,800 for head of household.
- **Dependent exemptions:** $1,000 per dependent.
- **Additional exemptions:** $700 each for taxpayers 65 or older and for blindness.
- **Limited deductions:** Massachusetts is stingy with deductions compared to the federal return — but it does allow items like the student loan interest deduction (up to federal limits), certain commuter and EZ-Pass deductions for eligible commuters, and contributions to the state's 529 plan (up to $1,000 single / $2,000 joint).

Massachusetts does **not** allow a standard deduction or itemized deductions the way the federal return does. Do not assume your federal deductions carry over — most do not.

## What is NOT taxed

- **Social Security benefits** are fully exempt from Massachusetts income tax.
- **Most government pensions** (Massachusetts state and municipal) are exempt.
- **Unemployment compensation** is taxable at the federal level but Massachusetts has periodically excluded it — check the current year's rules.
- Income below the **"no-tax status" thresholds** ($8,000 single, with higher limits for joint filers and heads of household, plus $1,000 per dependent) owes zero tax.

## 2026 filing essentials

- **Filing deadline:** April 15, 2027 for tax year 2026 (Massachusetts follows the federal deadline).
- **Filing threshold:** you must file if your gross income exceeds $8,000 (single) — lower than many people expect.
- **Estimated payments:** due April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15, same cadence as federal. You generally must prepay at least 80% of your eventual liability to avoid penalties — Massachusetts is stricter than the federal 90% figure people remember.
- **Free filing:** Massachusetts offers free e-filing for qualifying residents through its MassTaxConnect portal and the federal Free File program.

## A quick worked example

Single filer, 2026, $95,000 in wages, no other income:

1. Gross income: $95,000
2. Minus personal exemption: −$4,400 → taxable income $90,600
3. Tax at 5%: **$4,530**
4. Surtax: income is below $1,107,750 → $0

Effective state rate: about 4.77%. Now add a $1.3 million stock sale held 14 months: the gain faces 5% ($65,000) plus the 4% surtax on the $192,250 above the threshold ($7,690) — the surtax is real money at the top end.

Massachusetts rewards simplicity: one rate, a short list of exemptions, and a surtax that only touches very high incomes. Run your own scenario through the calculator, and if you are anywhere near the surtax threshold, talk to a tax professional before year-end — timing a large gain into January versus December can save thousands.
