Your GPA follows you everywhere in American education: college applications, scholarships, internships, grad school, even some first jobs. Yet most students can't explain exactly how theirs is calculated — they just trust whatever the transcript says.

Whether you're checking your school's math, figuring out what grades you need this semester to hit a 3.5, or planning around weighted AP classes, here's exactly how GPA calculation works in the US — and a free tool that does it for you.

## How the US 4.0 GPA scale works

Nearly every US high school and college converts letter grades to "grade points" on a 4.0 scale. Each letter grade is worth a fixed number of points:

| Grade | Points | Grade | Points |
|-------|--------|-------|--------|
| A / A+ | 4.0 | C+ | 2.3 |
| A− | 3.7 | C | 2.0 |
| B+ | 3.3 | C− | 1.7 |
| B | 3.0 | D+ | 1.3 |
| B− | 2.7 | D | 1.0 |
| — | — | F | 0.0 |

A couple of notes: some schools don't use plus/minus grades (straight A/B/C/D), and a few cap A+ at 4.0 rather than 4.3 — check your school's handbook if your numbers look slightly off. But this table covers the standard US system used by the vast majority of schools.

Crucially, **not all classes count equally**. A 4-credit lab course moves your GPA more than a 1-credit gym class. That's where credit hours come in.

## How to calculate GPA by hand (4 steps)

The formula: convert each grade to points, weight by credits, add everything up, divide by total credits.

**Worked example** — a semester with 4 classes:

| Class | Grade | Points | Credits | Quality points |
|-------|-------|--------|---------|----------------|
| English 101 | A− | 3.7 | 3 | 11.1 |
| Biology 101 | B+ | 3.3 | 4 | 13.2 |
| US History | B | 3.0 | 3 | 9.0 |
| Art | A | 4.0 | 1 | 4.0 |
| **Total** | | | **11** | **37.3** |

GPA = 37.3 ÷ 11 = **3.39**

The steps:

1. **Convert each final grade to grade points** using the table above.
2. **Multiply each class's points by its credit hours** — these are your "quality points."
3. **Add up all quality points** and **add up all credit hours attempted** (including failed classes — an F still counts in the divisor).
4. **Divide total quality points by total credits.** Round to two decimals — that's your semester GPA.

**Cumulative GPA** uses the same math across every semester: add all quality points you've ever earned and divide by all credits you've ever attempted. One bad semester hurts less than students fear, because it's diluted by everything else — which is also why one great semester can't fully erase a rough freshman year.

## Weighted vs. unweighted GPA: which one matters?

**Unweighted GPA** caps every class at 4.0 — an A in regular English and an A in AP Physics both count 4.0. It's the great equalizer, and it's what most colleges recalculate internally.

**Weighted GPA** rewards harder classes with extra points — typically up to 5.0 for AP/IB and 4.5 for honors. So an A in AP Chemistry might be worth 5.0 while an A in regular Chemistry is 4.0.

Which matters? Both, in different places:

- **Your high school rank** usually uses weighted GPA — that's why class rank rewards AP-heavy schedules.
- **College admissions** often recalculate your unweighted GPA themselves so they can compare applicants from different schools fairly — but they absolutely notice *how many* weighted classes you took. Rigor matters.
- **Scholarships** usually state which version they want. Read the fine print: a "3.5 GPA required" scholarship might mean unweighted.

The strategic takeaway: weighted GPA rewards challenging yourself, but only if you can handle it. A B in AP (3.0 unweighted, 4.0 weighted) versus an A in regular (4.0 both ways) is genuinely debatable — colleges say they prefer the B in AP, but a string of B's still drags the unweighted number they compare.

## 5 proven ways to raise your GPA

1. **Attack your highest-credit classes first.** GPA math rewards credits: turning a C into a B in a 4-credit science class moves the needle twice as much as the same jump in a 2-credit elective. Spend study hours proportionally.
2. **Use the syllabus as a battle plan.** Most professors publish exact grade weights. If the final is 40% and homework is 10%, that's where your hours go — not on color-coding the 10%.
3. **Fix the class, not just the grade.** Go to office hours in week 3, not week 13. Professors in US colleges are genuinely expected to help — and they remember who showed up when borderline grades get decided.
4. **Retake strategically.** Many schools let you retake a class and replace the grade (or average the two). One retaken D-to-B can lift a cumulative GPA by 0.1–0.2 — often the difference for scholarship cutoffs.
5. **Protect your GPA like money.** Because it literally is: the difference between a 3.0 and a 3.5 can be worth tens of thousands in merit scholarships. ## Frequently asked questions

**How do I calculate my GPA on a 4.0 scale?**
Convert each final grade to grade points (A = 4.0, B = 3.0, C = 2.0, D = 1.0, F = 0), multiply each by the course's credit hours, add up all the quality points, and divide by total credit hours attempted.

**What is the difference between weighted and unweighted GPA?**
Unweighted GPA caps every class at 4.0. Weighted GPA gives extra points (usually up to 5.0) for harder classes like AP, IB, and honors, rewarding students who take challenging coursework.

**Is a 3.5 GPA good?**
Yes — a 3.5 unweighted GPA is a solid B+/A- average that keeps most colleges, scholarships, and internships in reach. The average US high school GPA is around 3.0.

**Do colleges look at semester GPA or cumulative GPA?**
Both, but cumulative GPA (all semesters combined) matters most for admissions and scholarships. An upward trend across semesters also helps — colleges like to see improvement.
