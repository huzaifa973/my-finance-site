## Why a favicon matters

A favicon is branding at its smallest scale. When visitors have ten tabs open, your icon is how they find you again. It also appears in bookmarks, browser history, mobile home-screen shortcuts, and Google search results on mobile — each appearance reinforcing your identity.

Sites without a favicon look unfinished. It is one of those details visitors never consciously notice but that quietly signals "this site is maintained by someone who cares."

## How to create a favicon from text or emoji (step by step)

**1. Type your character.** Open the [favicon generator](/tools/favicon-from-text/) and type a single letter (usually your brand's initial), a short word, or paste an emoji. Emojis work beautifully as favicons — they are already colorful and recognizable at tiny sizes.

**2. Style it.** Pick a background color (or keep it transparent), choose the text color, and adjust the font style. Bold, simple shapes read best at 16x16 pixels — this is not the place for thin scripts or detail.

## Choosing between a letter and an emoji

**Letter favicons** are the professional default — think Google's "G" or Facebook's "f". A single bold initial in your brand colors is timeless and works for business sites, portfolios, and blogs.

**Emoji favicons** are the fast, playful option. They stand out in a sea of letters and are perfect for personal projects, tools, games, and casual brands. A rocket, a money bag, or a pizza slice is instantly memorable.

**Word favicons** (two or three letters) suit acronym brands. Keep it to two or three characters max — anything longer turns to mush at tab size.

## Tips for a favicon that reads well

Keep it bold. At 16 pixels wide, delicate details vanish. Thick strokes, high contrast, and generous letter sizing win.

Test on dark and light tabs. Browser themes vary — an icon that pops on a light tab may disappear on a dark one. Slightly saturated colors with good contrast work across both.

Use rounded backgrounds for letters. A solid rounded-square background in your brand color with a white letter is the most legible favicon formula there is.

## Favicon formats explained

You'll see a few file formats mentioned around favicons — here's what each does:

**PNG** is the modern default. It's supported everywhere, handles transparency, and looks crisp at every size. For most sites, PNG favicons are all you need.

**ICO** is the legacy format from the early web. Some very old systems still look for a `favicon.ico` file in the site root. If your generator offers it, including one costs nothing and maximizes compatibility.

**SVG** favicons are infinitely scalable and tiny in file size, and they can even adapt to dark mode with a media query. Browser support is good but not universal — use SVG as a bonus alongside PNG, not instead of it.

**Apple touch icons** (180x180 PNG) are what iPhones and iPads show when someone saves your site to their home screen. Without one, iOS takes an ugly screenshot thumbnail instead — so always export this size.

## Common favicon mistakes

**Too much detail.** A tiny landscape or intricate logo becomes an unrecognizable blob at 16 pixels. Simplify to one bold shape or letter.

**Skipping the 16px preview.** Your favicon mostly appears at tab size. If you only check the big preview, you'll miss legibility problems where they matter most.

**Forgetting mobile.** The Apple touch icon is what people see on their home screens — a missing one makes your site look broken when saved. Always export 180x180.

## Where your favicon appears

Your favicon shows up in more places than the browser tab: bookmark lists, browser history, address-bar autocomplete suggestions, mobile home-screen shortcuts, PWA install prompts, and even Google's mobile search results. Each appearance is a tiny brand impression — and a missing favicon in any of them quietly signals an unfinished site. Exporting the full size set once covers all of these surfaces permanently.
