Merging PDFs sounds simple until the combined file comes back with pages upside down, a missing chapter, or a 200 MB file nobody can email. This checklist walks you through the whole job — before, during, and after the merge — so the result is clean, correctly ordered, and easy to share. It is made for anyone who combines reports, invoices, application packs, scanned documents, or course materials, whether you merge files once a month or every day. Work through it top to bottom, and tick each step off as you go. The cost of skipping the checks is a file you have to rebuild under deadline pressure, or worse, a document a client or official receives with pages missing. Ten minutes with this checklist now saves that embarrassment later, and it turns merging from a chore you dread into a routine you trust.

## What's inside this resource

The checklist is split into five stages. Preparation covers gathering files and checking they open correctly. Organizing covers naming, ordering, and page orientation. The merge stage covers settings like bookmarks, page size, and compression. Verification catches the mistakes that slip through — wrong order, blank pages, broken links. The final stage covers sharing and archiving so the merged file stays useful long after you send it. Print the download or keep it beside your merging tool as a standard routine.

23. Choose a compression level: lighter for email, higher quality for print.

25. Keep hyperlinks working: verify links in the table of contents still jump correctly.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most common PDF merging mistake?

Wrong page order, followed by upside-down scanned pages. Both are caught by the organizing and verification stages of this checklist.

### Should I keep the original files after merging?

Yes, for at least 30 days. If a page turns out to be wrong or a recipient reports a problem, you can rebuild the merged file without re-scanning or re-exporting.

### Does merging change the quality of my PDFs?

It can, if you compress heavily. For print or formal use, choose high-quality settings; for email, a smaller file is usually worth the slight quality trade-off.
