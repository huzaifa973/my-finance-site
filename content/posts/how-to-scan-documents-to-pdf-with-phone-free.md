You don't need a scanner anymore. Your phone's camera, plus a free browser tool, produces PDFs that are perfectly fine for contracts, receipts, applications, and schoolwork. No app to install, no account to create, no watermark. Here's the full process.

## What you'll get

A proper multi-page PDF from your phone: flat, readable, right-side up, small enough to email. The kind of scan that looks professional instead of like a crooked photo of paper.

## Step 1: Photograph your documents

Good input makes good output. Spend 30 seconds getting this right:

1. **Lay the page flat** on a table or floor — not held in your hand, which curves the paper.
2. **Use bright, even light.** Daylight near a window is ideal. Overhead lights can cast a shadow from your phone; if you see one, shift the light or move the page.
3. **Hold the phone parallel to the page**, directly above the center. Fill the frame with the document, leaving a small margin around the edges.
4. **Tap to focus** on the text before shooting, and keep still. Blurry text can't be fixed later.
5. For multi-page documents, **photograph pages in order** — page 1 first, then page 2, and so on.

A quick tip: dark backgrounds (a dark table, or a sheet of dark paper underneath) help edge-detection tools find the page boundaries more accurately.

## Step 3: Check quality before you send

Open the finished PDF and zoom in on a paragraph. Ask yourself:

- **Is the text sharp?** If it's blurry, retake that page's photo — don't try to rescue it.
- **Is everything right-side up?** Rotate any page that came out sideways.
- **Is the file a reasonable size?** A few pages should be a couple of megabytes at most, fine for email attachments.

## Common scanning situations

### Receipts and small documents

Receipt paper curls and fades. Flatten it under a book for a minute first, and photograph in bright light. The contrast boost in the scan step matters most here.

### Thick books and bound documents

You can't lay a book flat without breaking the spine. Photograph one page at a time, holding the phone directly over each page, and keep the facing page out of frame. A slight page curve is fine — modern tools flatten it.

### IDs and cards

Place the card on a contrasting background and photograph both sides. Add both images as separate pages of one PDF so the front and back travel together.

### Handwritten notes

Handwriting scans best at high contrast. Make sure lighting is even (no half-shadow across the page) and let the enhancement step darken the ink.

## When to use a real scanner instead

Phone scanning handles 95% of everyday needs, but reach for an actual flatbed scanner (or a library's) when:

- You need archival-quality scans of photos or artwork.
- The document is very large (posters, blueprints).
- You're scanning hundreds of pages — a phone workflow gets tedious fast.

For everything else — the insurance form, the signed lease, the receipt for your expense report — your phone is plenty.

## Organizing your scans so you can find them later

Scanning is only half the job — a camera roll full of mystery PDFs helps nobody. Build a simple system:

- **Name files descriptively at creation:** `2026-10-lease-agreement.pdf` beats `scan-003.pdf` every time. Include the date and the document type.
- **Use folders by category:** Insurance, Taxes, Medical, Work, Receipts. Five folders cover most of life.
- **Back up the important ones.** Keep copies in two places — your phone plus a cloud drive or an external hard drive. Scanned copies of IDs, property deeds, and insurance policies deserve redundancy.
- **Shred or file the paper.** Once a scan is verified readable and backed up, decide: does the original need keeping? Tax documents and legal contracts usually do; old receipts and utility bills usually don't.

A ten-second naming habit today saves a frantic search the day you actually need the document.
