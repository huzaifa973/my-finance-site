Kansas keeps things refreshingly simple: **three brackets, three rates, unchanged since 2018** — and 2026 is no exception. The Kansas Department of Revenue confirmed that the state's contingent rate-reduction trigger did *not* activate for 2026 (revenues fell short of the inflation-adjusted threshold), so the familiar **3.1% / 5.25% / 5.7%** schedule stands. Here are the exact 2026 brackets for single and joint filers, how marginal rates actually work, a hand-calculation example, and the fastest way to get your number.

## Kansas 2026 tax brackets (single, head of household, married filing separately)

These apply to your **Kansas taxable income** — income after Kansas deductions and exemptions, not your gross salary:

| Kansas taxable income | Rate |
|---|---|
| First $15,000 | 3.1% |
| $15,001 – $30,000 | 5.25% |
| Over $30,000 | 5.7% |

## How marginal brackets work (the part everyone gets wrong)

Being "in the 5.7% bracket" does **not** mean you pay 5.7% on everything. Each rate applies only to the slice of income inside that bracket. A single filer with $40,000 of Kansas taxable income pays:

- 3.1% on the first $15,000 → **$465**
- 5.25% on the next $15,000 ($15,001–$30,000) → **$787.50**
- 5.7% on the remaining $10,000 ($30,001–$40,000) → **$570**
- **Total: $1,822.50** — an effective rate of about 4.6%, not 5.7%

This is why Kansas's system is gentler than it looks: most filers' effective rate lands well below the top marginal rate.


## Kansas deductions and exemptions: shrinking the taxable base

Before brackets even apply, Kansas lets you reduce taxable income:

- **Standard deduction** (Kansas-specific amounts, separate from federal)
- **Personal exemptions** for yourself, spouse, and dependents
- **Itemized deductions** if they beat the standard deduction

Strategy note: because Kansas's brackets are narrow — the top rate kicks in at just $30,000 single / $60,000 joint — deductions matter more here than in states with wide brackets. Maxing pre-tax contributions (401(k), HSA) and timing itemized deductions can keep more of your income in the 3.1% and 5.25% slices.

## Kansas vs. the neighbors

If you live near a state line, the difference is worth modeling — but remember that property and sales taxes shift the total picture.

## Kansas-specific tax breaks worth knowing

Beyond the standard deduction and exemptions, Kansas offers several credits and subtractions that directly cut the bill:

- **Food sales tax credit:** Kansas taxes groceries at a reduced rate and offers a credit aimed at lower-income households — check eligibility when you file.
- **Property tax relief programs:** homestead and senior/disabled property tax refunds reduce the total burden even though they are not income-tax items.
- **529 contributions:** Kansas allows a subtraction for contributions to its Learning Quest 529 plan, lowering Kansas taxable income dollar for dollar up to the annual limit.
- **Social Security:** Kansas exempts Social Security benefits for taxpayers under certain income thresholds — a meaningful break for retirees that phases in with income.

Stacking these with pre-tax retirement contributions is how Kansas filers legally keep the most income in the lower brackets.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are Kansas income tax brackets for 2026?

Kansas uses three brackets, unchanged since 2018: 3.1% on the first $15,000 of taxable income ($30,000 joint), 5.25% on income up to $30,000 ($60,000 joint), and 5.7% on income above that. Only the income inside each bracket is taxed at that rate.

### Did Kansas change its tax rates for 2026?

No. Kansas has a contingent rate-reduction law, but the Kansas Department of Revenue confirmed it did not trigger for 2026 because revenue collections did not exceed the inflation-adjusted threshold — so the 3.1% / 5.25% / 5.7% rates stand.

### What are the Kansas tax brackets for married couples filing jointly?

Joint filers get doubled thresholds: 3.1% on the first $30,000 of combined taxable income, 5.25% on income from $30,001 to $60,000, and 5.7% on income above $60,000.
