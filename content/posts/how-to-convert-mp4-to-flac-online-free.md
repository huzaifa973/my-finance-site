You have a video with great audio — a concert recording, a podcast episode, a lecture — and you want just the sound, kept at the highest quality possible. Converting MP4 to FLAC extracts the audio track and stores it in a lossless format: every bit preserved, nothing thrown away. Here is when that conversion makes sense, what quality you can honestly expect, and how to do it free in your browser.

## What "MP4 to FLAC" actually means

An MP4 is a container. Inside it there is usually a video stream and an audio stream — most often AAC audio, which is *lossy*: it was already compressed by throwing away audio data to save space.

Converting to FLAC does two things:

1. **Extracts** the audio stream out of the video container.
2. **Re-encodes** it as FLAC, which compresses the audio *without discarding anything*.

The critical fact: **FLAC cannot restore quality that AAC already removed.** If the MP4's audio was a 96 kbps AAC track, your FLAC will be a perfect copy of that 96 kbps-quality audio — just stored losslessly. What FLAC *does* guarantee is zero *additional* loss from this point on. Edit it, convert it, archive it: the audio you have today is the audio you keep.

## When FLAC is worth it (and when it is not)

**FLAC is worth it when:**
- You are **archiving** audio you care about and want a master copy that will never degrade through future conversions.
- You will **edit** the audio (podcast production, sampling, remixing) — every lossy re-encode stacks damage, so start from lossless.
- You are building a **music library** from video sources (live performances, rare recordings) and want the best available version preserved.

**FLAC is overkill when:**
- You just want to listen on your phone — a 256 kbps AAC or MP3 is audibly identical for most ears and one-tenth the size.
- Storage or bandwidth is tight — FLAC files run roughly 5–10x larger than the same audio as compressed AAC.
- The source audio was already low quality — lossless storage of a bad recording just preserves the badness faithfully.

A good rule of thumb: **FLAC for masters and editing, AAC/MP3 for listening copies.** Convert once to FLAC, then make smaller listening copies from it.

## Understanding FLAC file sizes

Newcomers are always surprised by how big FLAC files are. Rough expectations for stereo audio:

- **1 minute of CD-quality FLAC:** about 30–35 MB
- **A 3-minute song:** about 90–100 MB
- **A 1-hour podcast episode:** about 1.5–2 GB
- **A 2-hour concert video's audio:** about 3–4 GB

These are approximate — quiet, simple audio compresses smaller; dense, loud audio compresses larger. If a FLAC comes out suspiciously *small* (say, 10 MB for an hour), something went wrong and the audio was likely downsampled.

## MP4 to FLAC vs. other conversions

- **MP4 → MP3:** smaller files, but another round of lossy compression on already-lossy audio. Fine for casual listening, bad for archiving.
- **MP4 → WAV:** also lossless, but uncompressed — files roughly twice the size of FLAC with zero quality advantage. FLAC is the better archive format.
- **MP4 → FLAC:** the sweet spot for preservation — lossless like WAV, but compressed to about half the size.

## Common problems and fixes

- **"The FLAC sounds exactly the same as the MP4."** That is correct and expected — FLAC preserves, it does not enhance. If you wanted better sound, the limitation is the source recording, not the format.
- **Conversion is slow on long videos.** Browser-based conversion processes the whole file locally; a 2-hour video takes a few minutes. Close other heavy tabs to speed it up.
- **The output has no audio.** The MP4 may use an unusual audio codec the converter cannot decode. Try a different source file or convert the video to a standard MP4 first.
- **My music player won't play FLAC.** Most modern players (VLC, Foobar2000, Plex, recent Android/iOS apps) handle FLAC natively. Apple Music needs conversion to ALAC — a free, lossless side-step.

Converting MP4 to FLAC is the right move whenever the audio matters enough to keep forever. One conversion, zero further loss, and your concert recording or podcast master is preserved exactly as it sounded the day you extracted it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does converting MP4 to FLAC improve the audio quality?**
No. Conversion cannot restore quality lost to compression — FLAC preserves exactly the quality already in the MP4's audio track, with zero further loss. It prevents additional degradation; it does not reverse existing damage.

**Why is my FLAC file bigger than the original MP4?**
Because FLAC is lossless: it keeps every bit of audio data, while the MP4's AAC track discarded data to stay small. Expect FLAC files roughly 5–10x the size of the same audio as a 128 kbps AAC track.

**Can I convert MP4 to FLAC without losing quality?**
Yes — FLAC conversion is bit-perfect relative to the source track. The output contains the identical audio waveform the MP4 carried, just stored without lossy compression.

**What is the difference between MP4 audio and FLAC?**
MP4 usually carries AAC audio, a lossy format that shrinks files by discarding data. FLAC is a lossless format that compresses without discarding anything, so it is ideal for archiving and editing.
