PDFs are the closest thing we have to permanent digital documents — contracts, tax records, certificates, family history, research. But "saved as a PDF" is not the same as "archived." A PDF with missing embedded fonts opens as gibberish on another computer. A scan saved as an image-only PDF is unsearchable and useless if you ever need to find one clause in a thousand pages. Files on a single aging hard drive are one failure away from gone. Archiving means making deliberate choices so your documents stay readable, searchable, and intact for decades, not just until your next laptop upgrade.

This checklist walks you through the full process in five stages: preparing files for archiving (converting to PDF/A, embedding fonts, adding OCR), organizing them (naming conventions, folder structure, metadata), securing them (the 3-2-1 backup rule, checksums, access control), verifying them (integrity checks, readability tests), and maintaining the archive over time (migration schedules, format reviews). It's built for anyone with documents worth keeping — freelancers with client contracts, small businesses with tax records, families preserving important papers, researchers with source material. Work through it once to set up your archive properly, then run the maintenance section on a schedule.

## What's inside this checklist

The checklist has 30 items across five stages. Stage one, preparation, covers converting archival documents to PDF/A, embedding all fonts, running OCR on scans so every page is searchable, flattening form fields and annotations, and removing passwords and encryption that could lock you out later. Stage two, organization, sets up a consistent file naming convention, a logical folder structure, and embedded document metadata — title, author, subject, keywords — so files are findable without a separate database.

Stage three, secure storage, implements the 3-2-1 backup rule: three copies of everything, on two different types of media, with one copy offsite. It covers checksums for integrity verification, encryption for sensitive documents, and access controls so the wrong person can't delete or alter the archive. Stage four, verification, has you test that files open correctly on different software, confirm text is selectable and searchable, and validate PDF/A compliance. Stage five, maintenance, is the ongoing part most people skip: annual spot-checks, media migration every five to seven years, and a format review every decade to make sure PDF/A is still the right choice.

## How to use this checklist

1. Start with stage 1 on your most important documents first — contracts, tax records, certificates — then work outward to less critical files.
2. Convert to PDF/A and run OCR before organizing; it's far easier to rename and sort clean files than to reorganize a messy archive later.
3. Set up your naming convention and folder structure once, document it in the README, and never deviate — consistency is what makes an archive findable in ten years.
4. Implement the 3-2-1 backup rule before you consider the job done: an archive with one copy is just storage with optimism.
5. Put the maintenance dates in your calendar now — the annual spot-check and the 3-5 year full verification. Archives fail from neglect, not from bad formats.

Do the setup work once, properly, and your documents will outlive every device you own.

## Frequently asked questions

**What's the difference between PDF and PDF/A?**

PDF/A is a restricted, ISO-standardized subset of PDF designed for archiving. It requires embedded fonts, forbids encryption and external file references, and bans features that might not render in the future. A regular PDF can depend on fonts installed on your computer; a PDF/A carries everything it needs inside the file.

**Do I need special software to create PDF/A files?**

Most good PDF tools can save or export as PDF/A — look for "PDF/A" in the save or export options. Free tools including LibreOffice can do it. You don't need expensive archival software for a personal or small-business archive.

**How many documents make archiving worth the effort?**

Any document you'd be upset to lose permanently. Even a dozen critical files — property deeds, birth certificates, key contracts — deserve the PDF/A conversion and a second backup copy. The checklist scales down fine.
