## Why you'd split a PDF

Splitting isn't just about making files smaller. The most common reasons:

- **Send only what matters:** share chapter 4 of a textbook instead of the whole 800-page file, or send just the signed page of a contract.
- **Separate invoices and receipts:** an accounting export that lumps every monthly invoice into one file is far easier to file (and forward) as individual documents.
- **Shrink uploads:** portals for job applications, visas, and university admissions often cap attachments at 2–5 MB. Splitting a large PDF into parts is a quick way to get under the limit.
- **Extract a form:** pull the one application form buried inside a 60-page government document pack.
- **Fix mixed scans:** phone scanner apps sometimes save several documents as one PDF — splitting puts each document back where it belongs.

## Method 2: Built-in options on Windows and Mac

No extra tools needed — both major operating systems can extract pages through printing:

- **Windows:** open the PDF in Microsoft Edge or your browser, press Ctrl+P (Print), and under Pages select the range you want (e.g. 3–7). Set the printer to **Microsoft Print to PDF**, click Print, and save. Repeat for each part. It's tedious for many splits but works for a quick one-off.
- **Mac:** open the PDF in Preview, choose File → Print, enter the page range, then click the PDF button in the print dialog and select **Save as PDF**. Preview also lets you drag pages from the thumbnail sidebar into a new window and save that as a separate file.

These methods are fine for one or two extracts. For anything more involved, the browser tool above is much faster.

## Method 3: Adobe Acrobat

Adobe Acrobat (the paid desktop app) has the most polished splitting workflow: Organize Pages → Split, with options to split by number of pages, file size, or top-level bookmarks. If you already have a subscription for work, it's excellent.

The free Adobe Acrobat **online** splitter is more limited — it handles one file at a time, requires an Adobe account, and uploads your document to Adobe's servers. Fine for a public brochure; not ideal for anything sensitive.

## Mistakes to avoid when splitting

- **Scanned vs. text PDFs:** splitting a text-based PDF (real selectable text) preserves everything perfectly. Splitting a scanned PDF also works — but remember the scans are just images of text. If you need to search or copy text afterward, you'll need OCR on the output, not the original.
- **Losing bookmarks:** Acrobat's bookmark-based splitting keeps your document's table of contents intact. Simple range-based splitting usually drops bookmarks and internal links. If the document's navigation matters, keep the original file too.
- **Forgetting which range is which:** name your output files descriptively as you download them (e.g. `invoices-january.pdf` instead of `part-1.pdf`). It takes five seconds and saves a headache later.
- **Splitting before removing passwords:** a locked PDF can't be split by any tool. Unlock it first, then split the unlocked copy.

## Frequently asked questions

**How can I split a PDF for free?**
Open a free PDF splitter, load your file, choose the pages or ranges you want, and download each part as a new PDF. DollarWise's free split-PDF tool does this entirely in your browser, so nothing is uploaded anywhere.

**Does splitting a PDF reduce its quality?**
No. Splitting extracts existing pages without re-rendering them, so text, images, and layout stay exactly as they were. Quality only drops if you re-compress or convert the output afterward.

**Can I split an encrypted or password-protected PDF?**
Not until you unlock it. PDF tools can't read a file that's locked, so enter the password in your PDF reader first, save an unlocked copy, then split that copy.

**Is there a page limit for free PDF splitting?**
Browser-based splitters like DollarWise's have no page limit — the work happens on your device. Many server-based free tools cap you at 50 or 100 pages or throttle you to a few files per hour.
