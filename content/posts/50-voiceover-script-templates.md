A voiceover script is a performance document, not just words on a page. The same sentence can sound warm, urgent, playful, or authoritative depending on pacing, pauses, and emphasis — and a template that bakes those directions in saves you from a flat read and a stack of retakes.

These 50 templates cover the formats voice artists and creators use most: commercials, explainer videos, YouTube intros, podcast ads, audiobooks, e-learning, and phone systems. Each template includes the spoken lines plus simple direction notes in brackets, so you can hand it straight to a voice actor or record it yourself with confidence.

A few performance notes apply to nearly every template here. Mark your breaths in advance so you never gasp mid-sentence, and underline the one word in each sentence that carries the meaning — emphasizing the right word is half of good delivery. Record standing up if you can; it opens your breathing and adds energy you can hear. Finally, listen back on cheap earbuds or a phone speaker, because that is how most of your audience will hear you. If the read sounds clear and natural there, it will sound good everywhere.

## How to use this resource

Pick the template that matches your format, fill in the bracketed placeholders, and read it aloud twice before recording — once for flow, once for timing. Mark your own breaths and emphasis, record three takes, and choose the one that sounds like a real person talking, not someone reading. Keep the final script under your target length by reading at a natural pace, not by rushing.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a voiceover script be?

A good rule is about 150 words per minute of finished audio. A 30-second ad is roughly 75 words, and a 60-second ad about 150 words — always time a read-aloud before recording.

### Should I include direction notes in the script?

Yes. Notes like [pause] or [warm, smiling] help any voice actor match your intent and cut down on retakes. Keep them short and place them where the direction applies.

### Do I need a professional voice actor?

Not always. For internal videos, tutorials, and early content, your own voice is fine and often more authentic. For ads and brand work, a professional usually earns back the fee in quality.
