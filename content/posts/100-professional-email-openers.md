The first line of an email decides whether the rest gets read. A generic opener — 'I hope this email finds you well' — tells the reader nothing and signals that the message may not be worth their time. A specific opener, one that names their work, references a real conversation, or states a clear purpose, earns the next sentence. Professional email writing is not about fancy language; it is about respecting the reader's attention from the very first words.
This pack gives you 100 professional email openers across ten everyday situations: cold outreach, follow-ups, thank-you notes, introductions, requests, check-ins, apologies, congratulations, scheduling, and closings. Each opener has bracketed slots for names, companies, and topics, so you can adapt it in seconds. It suits office workers, freelancers, job seekers, and anyone who writes work email daily. ## How to use this resource

1. Pick the group that matches your situation, not the opener that sounds most impressive.
2. Replace every bracket with a real detail. An opener with placeholders still in it is worse than a plain one.
3. Keep the opener to one or two sentences. Its only job is to earn the next sentence.
4. Read the opener as the recipient: would it make you keep reading, or reach for delete?
5. Save your five most-used openers from the pack and adapt them, so work email gets faster without getting sloppier.

Good email openers are specific, short, and honest — three qualities no template can fake if you leave the brackets empty.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is 'I hope this email finds you well' really that bad?**

It is not offensive, just empty. It tells the reader nothing about why you are writing, and after the hundredth time, it signals a generic message. A specific opener earns attention faster.

**How long should an email opener be?**

One or two sentences. The opener earns the next sentence; it is not the message itself. Long openers bury the purpose of the email.

**Should openers be formal or friendly?**

Match the relationship. Formal for first contact and senior recipients, warmer for people you already know. Either way, be specific — warmth without specifics still reads as generic.
