Contracts get revised. Reports go through drafts. Invoices get "updated." And every time, somebody asks: *what actually changed?* Reading two 40-page PDFs side by side is nobody's idea of a good afternoon. A PDF comparison tool does it in seconds — here's how.

## What PDF comparison actually does

A compare tool extracts the text from both PDFs and runs a difference algorithm over them, producing a side-by-side view where:

- **Added text** is highlighted in one color (usually green).
- **Deleted text** is highlighted in another (usually red).
- **Modified passages** show the old and new wording next to each other.
- **Unchanged sections** can be collapsed, so you see only what moved.

It's the same concept as "track changes" in Word — except it works on any two PDFs, even ones you didn't author.

- Last month's report vs. this month's, to see what was quietly edited.

### Step 3: Review the differences

The tool presents the two texts side by side with changes highlighted. Work through it systematically:

1. **Scan the highlighted regions first** — these are the only parts that changed.
2. **Read modified clauses in full**, not just the highlighted words. A changed number ("30 days" → "90 days") can sit inside an otherwise identical sentence.
3. **Check the beginning and end.** Sneaky edits love the fine print: definitions sections, termination clauses, and signature blocks.
4. **Look for deletions.** Removed sentences don't jump out the way additions do — make sure nothing you cared about quietly vanished.

### Step 4: Save or export the comparison

If the tool offers an export (a marked-up report or a list of changes), save it. A dated record of "here's exactly what changed between v1 and v2" is gold in any dispute.

## Limitations to know about

**Scanned PDFs won't compare.** If your PDF is just images of pages (common with scanned contracts), there's no text to extract. Run it through OCR first, then compare the OCR'd versions.

**Formatting changes may not show.** Most text-based comparison focuses on words, not fonts, colors, or layout. If someone changed a table's numbers, that shows; if they changed a logo, it won't.

**Very long documents take longer.** A 200-page comparison still runs, but give it a moment — and review the change summary before diving into every highlighted line.

**Tables and columns can confuse extraction.** Text pulled from complex layouts may read out of order. The differences are still detected, but the side-by-side view might look jumbled on heavily formatted pages. Compare the extracted text carefully in those spots.

## A smart workflow for version control

If you handle documents regularly, make comparison part of your routine:

1. **Name versions clearly:** `contract-acme-v1.pdf`, `contract-acme-v2.pdf` — never "final" and "final-final".
2. **Diff before every signature.** No exceptions, even with trusted parties. Mistakes happen.
3. **Archive the diff report** alongside both versions. Future-you will thank present-you.
4. **Keep the originals.** The comparison is a guide; the source files are the record.

## Red flags to watch for in compared documents

Once you make diffing a habit, you'll start noticing patterns. Watch especially for these:

- **Changed numbers in payment terms.** Amounts, dates, and percentages are the most consequential single-character edits in any document — "net 30" becoming "net 60" or a fee moving from 2% to 3% changes real money.
- **Softened language.** "Shall" becoming "may," "guarantee" becoming "aim to," or a warranty period quietly shortening. These edits rarely get announced.
- **New clauses at the end.** Fresh paragraphs appended near the signature block — arbitration clauses, auto-renewal terms, or fee schedules that weren't in the version you reviewed.
- **Reordered sections.** Sometimes nothing is added or removed, but clauses are moved — which can change how provisions reference each other. Read the surrounding text of anything that shifted position.

None of these are necessarily malicious; people revise documents for legitimate reasons all the time. But each one deserves a conscious read before you sign, and a comparison tool is what makes that read practical instead of punishing.
